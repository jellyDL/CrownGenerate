\begin{abstract}
%% Text of abstract 
%% Todo：后期需要精简到 250 词内
Personalized dental crown design is a critical step in digital restorative workflows for structurally compromised teeth, requiring the recovery of detailed anatomy while ensuring compatibility with the preparation margin, adjacent teeth, and opposing dentition. Conventional computer-aided design (CAD) workflows require dental technicians to repeatedly adjust template crowns, making the process time-consuming and highly dependent on operator experience. To address this limitation, we propose a case-conditioned 3D generative method for tooth-supported posterior single crowns. Given full-arch maxillary and mandibular scans, a manually delineated three-dimensional preparation margin, and the FDI tooth number, the method directly generates an editable crown mesh. It employs an oriented signed flexible dual-grid representation to jointly encode crown geometry, signed distances, and oriented normals, while a sparse variational autoencoder compresses high-resolution geometry into a compact latent space. Structure and feature flows sequentially generate occupancy and local surface features. Multiscale dental condition injection and surface supervision during decoding preserve anatomical details, marginal continuity, and spatial coordination within the dentition. Experiments on an in-house dataset showed that the proposed method outperformed baseline approaches in both geometric reconstruction and spatial coordination. Blinded assessments by dental technicians further indicated superior crown quality and practical usability. These findings demonstrate the potential of the proposed method to improve design efficiency and generate case-adapted CAD starting designs for posterior single-crown restorations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}
\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}

\hspace{2em}The integration of intraoral scanners (IOS) into clinical practice has revolutionized the acquisition of high-precision 3D dental models, offering sub-millimeter accuracy in digital representations of dental arches \cite{ahmed2024digital, cerghizan2025accuracy}. Automated and accurate 3D tooth segmentation provides fundamental support for orthodontic monitoring and disease localization \cite{eggmann2024recent}. They facilitate the creation of personalized treatment plans and deliver detailed 3D models. This automation enhances clinical efficiency and reduces the time needed compared to manual methods. However, the automation of 3D dental image segmentation continues to present significant challenges. Clinical complexities such as diverse tooth morphologies, inter-tooth crowding, misalignment, scanning noise, missing teeth, and complex shapes substantially compromise the effectiveness and reliability of segmentation algorithms.

\hspace{2em}A spectrum of fully automated instance segmentation methods for 3D dental meshes has been developed, encompassing both traditional methods and deep learning-based ones. Traditional geometry-based methods often rely on geometric features and prior knowledge of tooth shapes, including curvature-based approaches \cite{miyazaki2020teeth}, clustering dental point clouds \cite{deng2020dbscan}, and the harmonic field method \cite{zhang2020automatic}. However, these traditional geometric methods encounter significant challenges when dealing with complex tooth arrangements and morphological variations. They tend to be sensitive to noise and artifacts in the data, which can lead to inaccurate segmentation results.

\hspace{2em}Meanwhile, deep learning-based dental technology has emerged, marking not only a profound integration of artificial intelligence (AI) with oral medicine but also a pivotal milestone in the transition from digital dentistry to intelligent dentistry~\cite{tian2021efficient,tian2021dcpr,zhao2026innovative}. These methods can be principally divided into point-cloud, surface, and graph-based methods. Point-based methods \cite{tian20223d,mao2024cursegnet,wu2024point} directly process discrete point-cloud data, using coordinates and normal vectors to learn boundary features. Graph-based methods \cite{liu2024mesh, farazi2025recipe}, in contrast, extract features from tooth surfaces or patches. By leveraging network structures like graph convolutional networks (GCN) to incorporate the spatial and structural information of tooth surfaces, these methods provide a more comprehensive representation for boundary feature extraction and analysis.

\hspace{2em}Despite these advancements, current deep learning-based approaches exhibit critical limitations in capturing local boundary details while preserving global contextual information, resulting in insufficient precision for clinical applications~\cite{li2023position}. Several dual-branch and fusion-based architectures have attempted to reconcile this trade-off~\cite{wang2026style}; however, they either require labor-intensive post-processing that precludes end-to-end optimization~\cite{zheng2022teethgnn}, or fail to adaptively balance global and local features in challenging scenarios~\cite{wu2022two,wang2024graph}. To address these limitations, we introduce PASegNet, a novel dual-branch framework for enhanced 3D tooth segmentation. Unlike prior methods that treat global and local features as independent tasks, our PASegNet integrates a position-aware branch that leverages global contextual information to model spatial relationships between adjacent teeth and surrounding structures, guiding accurate centroid identification through activation maps. Meanwhile, a boundary-aware branch focuses on local geometry to capture fine-grained details beyond simple coordinates and normals, ensuring precise delineation of tooth boundaries. Additionally, PASegNet employs an improved attention-guided fusion module that adaptively adjusts the contributions of each branch by balancing the weights of different modules, further enhancing its ability to handle challenging dental irregularities.

\hspace{2em}The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

\vspace{0.1em}

\hspace{1em}• PASegNet is proposed as an innovative end-to-end framework for precise instance segmentation of teeth in 3D dental models. It features a dual-branch architecture that simultaneously provides tooth location information and 3D mesh segmentation results.

\hspace{1em}• An improved AFM integrated with a dynamic attention mechanism is introduced. This strategy adaptively adjusts the contributions of each branch by balancing the weights of different modules, thereby enhancing the model's capacity to handle complex dental scenarios and ensuring more accurate segmentation.

\hspace{1em}• Multiple loss functions, including centroid-guided loss, local region loss, and boundary-constrained loss, are designed. These functions focus on different aspects of the features, ensuring that both tooth regional information and boundary details are effectively captured.

\hspace{1em}• The method is validated on the public datasets Teeth3DS \cite{ben2022teeth3ds} and 3DTeethSeg \cite{ben20233dteethseg}. Experiments demonstrate that the method outperforms existing methods in terms of segmentation accuracy and robustness, making it a promising solution for clinical applications in 3D dental model segmentation.

\section{Related work}\label{sec2}

\hspace{2em}In the realm of 3D tooth segmentation, various methodologies have been proposed to address the complex geometric structures and diverse shapes of teeth. These approaches can be broadly categorized into point-based methods, surface and graph-based methods, and multi-view methods, each with its unique advantages and challenges.

%% 2.1
\subsection{Point Cloud-based Methods}\label{subsec2_1}

\hspace{2em}Point cloud-based methods have been widely used in 3D tooth segmentation due to their ability to directly process raw point cloud data. Qiu et al.~\cite{qiu2022darch} proposed a weakly annotated training approach to train the segmentation network using only a few annotated teeth from each dental model. Through the integration of an initial feature extractor and an attention-weighted local aggregator into PointNet++, Yuan et al.~\cite{yuan2023full} achieved precise delineation of the tooth--gingival margin. Recently, Transformers have advanced point cloud analysis: Point Transformer evolved from self-attention to grouped attention and serialized mapping~\cite{wu2025spiking}, while Mamba3D~\cite{han2024mamba3d} and Point Cloud Mamba~\cite{zhang2025point} emerged as linear-complexity alternatives via space-filling curves and bidirectional modeling. In dentistry, Wu et al.~\cite{wu2024transformer} proposed PointRegion, a Transformer-based 3D tooth segmentation network that partitions the point cloud into regions to reduce computational complexity and memory usage. Xi et al.~\cite{xi20253d} introduced CrossTooth, extending the Point Transformer architecture with curvature-based vertex retention to strengthen hierarchical attention modeling at crown-gingiva junctions.

\hspace{2em}At the same time, point cloud-based methods face several challenges. First, point cloud data is irregular and sparse, making it difficult to capture the complete and accurate structure of teeth, especially in areas with few points. Second, these methods struggle to effectively represent local geometric details due to the lack of inherent local structure information. Third, point cloud processing is computationally intensive due to the large amount of data, necessitating downsampling techniques that often lead to loss of fine-grained details critical for precise margin delineation. Furthermore, performance is highly dependent on data characteristics such as resolution and noise levels from different intraoral scanners. Most critically, existing point-based methods including the recent transformer and Mamba architectures primarily focus on general 3D scenes and lack mechanisms specifically designed for capturing the intricate boundary characteristics between adjacent teeth, which is essential for clinically accurate tooth segmentation.

%% 2.2
\subsection{Surface and Graph-based Methods}\label{subsec2_2}

\hspace{2em}Surface and graph-based methods have also shown promise in this field. Early works include iMeshSegNet~\cite{wu2022two}, which captures multi-scale features via vertex attributes, and STSNet~\cite{liu2022hierarchical}, employing self-supervised pre-training for label-efficient learning. These approaches were later complemented by dilated convolutions for enlarged receptive fields~\cite{krenmayr2024dilatedtoothsegnet} and multi-stage geometric processing strategies~\cite{rekik2025tseglab}. Recently, this paradigm has shifted toward transformer architectures, initially through general-purpose mesh segmentation methods (MeT~\cite{vecchio2023met}, GeoTransformer~\cite{farazi2025recipe}), and subsequently to dental applications where Duan et al.~\cite{duan20233d} pioneered graph transformer modules to significantly improve boundary classification accuracy.

\hspace{2em}These surface and graph-based approaches have offered fresh perspectives for 3D tooth segmentation. Yet, these methods also encounter hurdles like the requirement for accurate graph construction and the computational complexity of graph-based models~\cite{wang2025distilling,wang2024breast}. Moreover, existing mesh transformers designed for general shapes lack specialized mechanisms for dental applications, particularly regarding precise boundary delineation. Future work should focus on dental-specific architectures that jointly leverage global positional information and fine-grained local geometric details.

%% 2.3
\subsection{Multi-view Methods}\label{subsec2_3}
\hspace{2em}Owing to the widespread use of 2D image recognition, multi-view methods project 3D teeth models onto multiple 2D views and then transform the results back to 3D space for segmentation. Huang et al. \cite{huang2024iossam} proposed IOSSAM, a multi-view method that uses occlusal and dental crown prompters to generate segmentation prompts. It converts 2D results into 3D via view-aware label diffusion, enhancing tooth segmentation accuracy and robustness with limited labeled data. Similarly, Tibor Kubík et al. \cite{kubik2024lmvsegrnn} presented LMVSegRNN, which captures intricate tooth shapes and spatial relationships by leveraging unique features and complementary information from different views. This method is advantageous for segmenting teeth in unusual positions, rotated, or closely located.

\hspace{2em}Nevertheless, multi-view methods have several limitations. Projecting 3D data into multiple 2D views can lead to information loss between different views, which may affect the accuracy of the final segmentation results. These methods also require careful alignment and registration of the multiple views to ensure consistency. Overall, multi-view methods offer a unique approach to 3D tooth segmentation.

\hspace{2em}Existing 3D tooth segmentation methods struggle to simultaneously capture local details and global context, causing inaccuracies in boundary delineation and tooth localization. These methods suffer from decoupled processing without adaptive integration. In contrast, our PASegNet bridges this gap through a dual-branch architecture inspired by Wang et al.~\cite{wang2024graph}: one branch captures position-aware global features for robust tooth localization, while the other extracts HKS-based boundary-sensitive details via spectral geometry. Unlike existing approaches that process these hierarchies independently, our attention-guided fusion module dynamically balances both streams, enabling simultaneous exploitation of mesh topology and dental-specific boundary awareness. This unified design outperforms both single-branch transformers and conventional dual-branch architectures by achieving adaptive coupling between global positioning and local geometry, ensuring superior segmentation accuracy and robustness in challenging clinical scenarios.

\section{Method}\label{sec3}

%% 3.1
\subsection{Overview}\label{subsec3_1}

\hspace{2em}Fig. \ref{fig:figure_overall_architecture} presents the overall framework of PASegNet. The network takes vertices’ coordinates (N × 3 matrices) and heat kernel signature (HKS) \cite{sun2009concise} features (N × 16 matrices) as inputs, where N is the total number of vertices. Each mesh element is annotated as one of C = 33 semantic classes, and the network outputs an N × C probability matrix for segmenting the mesh. The end-to-end architecture comprises two branches: the G branch with the Position-Aware Module (PAM) for global context capture and the L branch with the Boundary-Aware Module (BAM) for local detail extraction. The Attention-guided Fusion Module (AFM) integrates information from both branches to balance global and local features. 

\hspace{2em}Different from existing methods \cite{mao2024cursegnet,liu2024geo,rekik2025tseglab}, the above-mentioned improvements enable simultaneous processing of global tooth-position info and local boundary details, effectively handling complex dental cases such as missing teeth and crowding. The following sections will provide detailed introductions to these modules.

%% 3.2
\subsection{Position-Aware Module}\label{subsec3_2}

\hspace{2em}Due to the varying sparsity of the input mesh, we apply classical Farthest Point Sampling (FPS)~\cite{eldar1997farthest} only in the position-aware G branch, where uniform spatial coverage improves learning of inter-tooth spatial relationships and reduces computational cost. Anatomically critical boundary vertices are preserved by the boundary-aware L branch, which processes full-resolution HKS features without downsampling. The encoder is inspired by the Dynamic Graph CNN (DGCNN) \cite{wang2019dynamic}, using EdgeConv to dynamically construct neighborhood graphs that capture local neighborhoods for robust global feature extraction. The feature extraction process in EdgeConv can be mathematically expressed as:

\begin{equation}
f_k = \max_{j \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \left( \theta \left( \|x_i - x_j\| \right) \right)
\end{equation}

where \(\mathcal{N}(i)\) denotes the dynamically constructed neighborhood of point \(i\), and \(\theta\) is a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) that transforms the edge features. Here, \(x_i\) and \(x_j\) represent the feature vectors of the central point and its neighbor, respectively. The operation \(\|x_i - x_j\|\) captures the relative positional relationship between points, enabling the network to learn both local geometric structures and spatial dependencies.

\hspace{2em}Among architectures for global feature extraction, we systematically evaluated EdgeConv against attention-only mechanisms and traditional graph convolution networks. We selected EdgeConv for the following reasons: (1) Dynamic adaptability: EdgeConv dynamically computes $k$-nearest neighbors in the feature space, adapting to varying densities and geometric configurations inherent. (2) Computational efficiency: Compared to self-attention mechanisms with $\mathcal{O}(N^2)$ complexity, EdgeConv maintains $\mathcal{O}(NK)$ complexity where $K$ is the number of neighbors, making it suitable for high-resolution dental scans. 

\hspace{2em}To aggregate local features and learn global shape features, we stack multiple EdgeConv layers. This feature aggregation process can be formulated using a hierarchical graph pooling mechanism:

\begin{equation}
G^{(l)} = \bigcup_{k=1}^{K^{(l)}} f_k^{(l)}
\end{equation}

where \(G^{(l)}\) represents the global features at the \(l\)-th layer, and \(K^{(l)}\) is the number of features at that layer.

\hspace{2em}As shown in Fig. \ref{fig:figure_DGCNN_features}, we utilized Grad-CAM-based \cite{selvaraju2020grad} visualization to display the feature activation maps of cascading EdgeConv layers (x1, x2, and x3). By calculating the standard deviation of each point's features relative to its neighbors, we created a global feature quantification metric. A smaller standard deviation indicates more globalized features. The global feature indicator distribution, highlighted by brighter areas in the visualization, demonstrates the network's shift in focus from local to global features. Guided by this information, we found that reducing the number of EdgeConv layers still allows for effective global information extraction. To enhance feature extraction efficiency, our approach simplified the DGCNN structure by using only three cascaded EdgeConv layers.

\hspace{2em}Subsequently, a cascade of MLP layers progressively reduces dimensionality to extract high-level global features. This dimensionality reduction can be formulated as a nonlinear transformation:

\begin{equation}
F = \text{MLP}(G) = \sigma \left( W \cdot \text{BN}(G) + b \right)
\end{equation}

where \(W\) and \(b\) are learnable parameters, \(\text{BN}(\cdot)\) denotes batch normalization, and \(\sigma\) is the activation function.

This innovative approach allows the model to efficiently capture the global information of tooth positions, significantly enhancing the accuracy and robustness of 3D tooth segmentation.

\hspace{2em}Positional information plays a crucial role in coordinate-dependent computer vision tasks by enabling neural networks to learn spatial relationships and structural constraints. In our task, positional data facilitates the accurate identification of tooth numbers among adjacent teeth and enables precise delineation of boundaries between individual teeth \cite{krenmayr2024dilatedtoothsegnet}.

\subsection{Boundary-Aware Module}\label{subsec3_3}

\hspace{2em}Accurately segmenting the boundaries of individual teeth has always been a major challenge in this field \cite{gong2021boundary}. Relying solely on coordinates and normals makes it difficult to obtain sufficient local information. To solve this problem, we introduced the HKS as a relative regional embedding, which is a geometry-based feature. Based on heat diffusion, HKS can accurately capture the local geometric information of 3D shapes and better grasp the local geometric details of teeth. For a vertex \( v \) on the mesh, the HKS is defined as a function in a temporal domain:
\begin{equation}
\text{HKS}(v, t) = \sqrt{\frac{\phi_0(v)}{4\pi t}} \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} e^{-\lambda_i t} \phi_i(v)^2
\end{equation}

where \( t \) represents different time scales. \( \lambda_i \) and \( \phi_i(v) \) denote the eigenvalues of the Laplace–Beltrami operator and their associated eigenfunctions evaluated at vertex \( v \).

\hspace{2em}We chose HKS over curvature-based embeddings and spectral descriptors for following reasons: (1) Multi-scale representation: Different from single-scale curvature features (Gaussian or mean curvature), HKS captures geometric information across multiple scales through the time parameter $t$, providing richer boundary characterization. (2) Noise robustness: Curvature features are sensitive to mesh noise and irregularities, whereas HKS demonstrates superior stability due to its diffusion-based formulation. (3) Intrinsic property: HKS is intrinsic to the surface geometry and invariant to isometric deformations, making it robust to scanning artifacts. (4) Computational tractability: Compared to spectral descriptors like Wave Kernel Signature (WKS)~\cite{aubry2011wave} that require extensive eigendecomposition, HKS can be efficiently approximated via truncated eigenvalue expansion.

\hspace{2em}To further enhance the precision of boundary delineation, we combine HKS features with graph structures \cite{bhatti2023deep}. This is achieved by constructing a graph \( G = (V, E) \), where \( V \) represents the vertices of the dental model, and \( E \) represents the edges connecting neighboring vertices. The HKS features are then propagated through the graph using a graph convolution operation:

\begin{equation}    
    H^{(l+1)} = \text{GCN}(H^{(l)}, A) = \sigma \left( \tilde{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}} \tilde{A} \tilde{D}^{-\frac{1}{2}} H^{(l)} W^{(l)} \right)
\end{equation}

Here, \( H^{(l)} \) is the feature matrix at layer \( l \), \( \tilde{A} = A + I \) is the adjacency matrix with added self-connections, \( \tilde{D} \) is the degree matrix of \( \tilde{A} \), and \( W^{(l)} \) is the learnable weight matrix at layer  \( l \). By integrating HKS features with graph structures, the BAM can effectively utilize both local geometric details and structural relationships.

%% 3.4
\subsection{Attention-guided Fusion Module}\label{subsec3_4}

\hspace{2em}AFM serves as the critical integration component, dynamically integrating global and local features to address complex dental segmentation challenges. Fig. \ref{fig:fusion_module_architecture} illustrates its detailed architecture. Features from both branches are processed through a transformer-based mechanism, enabling adaptive feature weighting based on geometric contexts to handle diverse morphological variations in dental structures.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth, keepaspectratio]{aff_frame.pdf}
    \caption{Architecture of the proposed Attention-guided Fusion Module. The module takes local features, global features, and vertex positions as input, and outputs fused features that effectively combine local geometric details with global contextual information through dynamic attention mechanisms.}\label{fig:fusion_module_architecture}
\end{figure*}

\hspace{2em}The module takes three inputs: global features from the G Branch for overall shape understanding, local features from the L Branch for vertex-level geometric details, and 3D coordinates for geometry-aware position encoding. The AFM consists of three core components: Geometry-aware Position Encoding, Additive Feature Fusion, and Dynamic Multi-Head Attention, each described in detail below.

\bigskip

\textbf{Geometry-aware Position Encoding.} Standard Transformer architectures~\cite{hassija2025transformers} rely on self-attention mechanisms but inherently lack spatial awareness. Conventional position encoding methods, including sinusoidal functions and learned embeddings, are designed for sequential or grid-structured data and fail to capture the complex 3D spatial relationships essential for dental mesh analysis. To address this limitation, we propose a geometry-aware position encoding mechanism that directly leverages 3D vertex coordinates:

\begin{equation} \mathbf{P} = \mathbf{V} \mathbf{W}_{pos} + \mathbf{b}_{pos} \end{equation}

where $\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 3}$ represents vertex coordinates, $\mathbf{W}_{pos} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times {d}_{G}}$ and $\mathbf{b}_{pos} \in \mathbb{R}^{{d}_{G}}$ are learnable position encoding parameters, $d_G$ is the global feature dimension. This formulation enables the model to learn geometric embeddings suited for complex dental surface representation.


% 伪代码
\begin{algorithm}[t]
    \SetAlgoLined
    \SetKwInOut{KwInput}{Input}
    \SetKwInOut{KwOutput}{Output}
    \SetKwInOut{KwParams}{Parameters}
    
    \caption{Attention-guided Fusion Module (AFM)}
    \label{alg:afm}
    \KwInput{$\mathbf{F}_L \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d_L}$ (local feature), 
             $\mathbf{F}_G \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d_G}$ (global feature), 
             $\mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 3}$ (vertex coordinate)}
    \KwOutput{$\mathbf{F}_{\text{out}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times C}$ (fused feature)}
    \KwParams{$h$ (initial number of attention heads, default $h=8$)}

    \tcp{Stage 1: Feature Projection and Geometry-aware Encoding (Corresponds to Fig. 4 left)}
    $\mathbf{P} \leftarrow \mathbf{V} \cdot \mathbf{W}_{\text{pos}} + \mathbf{b}_{\text{pos}}$ \tcp*{Geometry-aware position encoding}
    $\mathbf{F}_{\text{combined}} \leftarrow \mathbf{F}_L + \mathbf{F}_G + \mathbf{P}$ \tcp*{Additive feature fusion}
    
    \tcp{Stage 2: Dynamic Multi-Head Attention (Corresponds to Fig. 4 lower-right)}
    \eIf{$d_G \bmod h \neq 0$}{
        $h^* \leftarrow \max\left(1, \frac{d_G}{\lfloor d_G/h \rfloor + 1}\right)$ \tcp*{Dynamic head adjustment}
    }{
        $h^* \leftarrow h$ \tcp*{Use initial head count}
    }
    $d_h \leftarrow d_G / h^*$ \tcp*{Dimension per head}

    % $\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V} \leftarrow \text{Linear}(\mathbf{F}_{\text{combined}}) \times 3$ \tcp*{Projections}
    \tcp{Linear projections for Q, K, V}
    $\mathbf{Q} \leftarrow \mathbf{F}_{\text{combined}} \mathbf{W}_Q, \quad \mathbf{K} \leftarrow \mathbf{F}_{\text{combined}} \mathbf{W}_K, \quad \mathbf{V} \leftarrow \mathbf{F}_{\text{combined}} \mathbf{W}_V$\;
    
    \tcp{Multi-head attention computation}
    \For{$i = 1$ \KwTo $h^*$}{
        $\text{Attn}_i \leftarrow \text{softmax}\left(\mathbf{Q}_i \mathbf{K}_i^\top / \sqrt{d_h}\right) \cdot \mathbf{V}_i$\;
    }
    $\mathbf{F}_{\text{attn}} \leftarrow \text{Concat}(\text{Attn}_1, \ldots, \text{Attn}_{h^*}) \cdot \mathbf{W}_O$\; 
    
    \tcp{Stage 3: Residual Connection and Feed-Forward (Corresponds to Fig. 4 right)}
    $\mathbf{F}_{\text{norm}} \leftarrow \text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{F}_{\text{attn}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{combined}})$ \tcp*{Residual + LayerNorm}
    $\mathbf{F}_{\text{ffn}} \leftarrow \text{Linear}_{\text{out}}(\text{ReLU}(\text{Linear}_{\text{in}}(\mathbf{F}_{\text{norm}})))$ \tcp*{Feed-Forward}
    $\mathbf{F}_{\text{out}} \leftarrow \text{LayerNorm}(\mathbf{F}_{\text{ffn}} + \mathbf{F}_{\text{norm}})$ \tcp*{Final residual + LayerNorm}
    
    \Return{$\mathbf{F}_{\text{out}}$}
\end{algorithm}


\textbf{Additive Feature Fusion.} An additive feature-fusion strategy is introduced for efficient integration of multi-source features while preserving dimensional consistency:

\begin{equation} \mathbf{F}_{combined} = \mathbf{F}_L + \mathbf{F}_G + \mathbf{P} \end{equation}

Here, $\mathbf{F}_L$, $\mathbf{F}_G$, and $\mathbf{P}$ denote the local feature, global feature, and position-encoded feature, respectively. Compared to concatenation-based fusion, the proposed strategy preserves feature dimensions while enabling direct channel-wise interaction and maintaining computational efficiency, thereby achieving an optimal balance between performance and cost without parameter inflation.

\bigskip

\textbf{Dynamic Multi-Head Attention.} The details of the module are shown in the lower-right corner of Fig. \ref{fig:fusion_module_architecture}. This module takes the fused feature $\mathbf{F}_{\text{combined}}$ as input and employs the standard attention mechanism. For each vertex $i$, we compute the query, key, and value vectors through linear projections:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Q}_i = \mathbf{F}_{\text{combined}}[i] \cdot \mathbf{W}_Q, \quad \mathbf{K}_i = \mathbf{F}_{\text{combined}}[i] \cdot \mathbf{W}_K, \quad \mathbf{V}_i = \mathbf{F}_{\text{combined}}[i] \cdot \mathbf{W}_V
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_Q, \mathbf{W}_K, \mathbf{W}_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d_G \times d_h}$ are learnable projection matrices, and $d_h$ denotes the dimension per attention head.

\hspace{2em}The core innovation lies in dynamically adjusting the number of attention heads $h^*$, based on the feature dimension $d_G$:
\begin{equation}
h^* = \max\left(1, \left\lfloor \frac{d_G}{h} \right\rfloor + 1\right) \quad \text{if } d_G \bmod h \neq 0
\end{equation}
where $h$ is the initial head count. This mechanism ensures that the number of attention heads is dynamically adjusted to maintain compatibility with the feature dimensions, optimizing computational efficiency and model performance.

\hspace{2em}The AFM dynamically balances contributions between global positional information and local geometric details, treating the centroid heatmap as a complementary prior. In complex dentitions such as crowded teeth, it adaptively refines coarse localization with boundary features, reducing centroid reliance when local boundaries indicate clearer separation, thereby resolving ambiguities and semantic inconsistencies. The detailed algorithmic procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:afm}.

\subsection{Loss Function}\label{subsec3_5}

\hspace{2em}We introduce three efficient loss functions to further enhance performance, including centroid-guided loss \cite{zhao2024few}, local region loss, and boundary-constrained loss \cite{tan2023boundary}. These functions can effectively improve the accuracy and robustness of segmentation models.

\bigskip

\textbf{Centroid Guided Loss.} The centroid guided loss is designed to accurately locate tooth centroids. It is defined as:

\begin{equation} 
L_{centroid}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N\left\|C_i-\hat{C}_i\right\|^2
\end{equation}
where \( N \) denotes the number of teeth. \( C_{i} \) corresponds to the reference centroid of the \( i-th \) tooth. While \(\hat{C}_{i}\) signifies the predicted centroid of the \( i-th \) tooth.

\bigskip

\textbf{Local Region Loss.} Local region loss focuses on precise segmentation within local regions. It is defined as:
\begin{equation}
L_{local}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=1}^M\left(1-\frac{2\cdot TP_j}{TP_j+FP_j+FN_j}\right)
\end{equation}
where \( M \) is the number of local regions, \( TP_j \) refers to the true positive count for the \( j-th \) local region, \( FP_j \) denotes the false-positive count within the \( j-th \) local region, and \( FN_j \) denotes the false-negative count within the \( j-th \) local region.

\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\hspace{2em}This loss function aims to maximize the Dice coefficient within each local region, ensuring precise segmentation.

\bigskip

\textbf{Boundary-Constrained Loss.} The boundary-constrained loss ensures clear and accurate delineation of tooth boundaries. It is defined as
\begin{equation}
L_{boundary}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^K\left(1-\frac{\left|B_k\cap\hat{B}_k\right|}{\left|B_k\cup\hat{B}_k\right|}\right)
\end{equation}
where \( K \) is the number of boundaries, \( B_k \) is associated with the ground-truth boundary of the \( k-th \) tooth, and \(\hat{B}_{k}\) pertains to the predicted boundary of the \( k-th \) tooth.

\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\hspace{2em}This loss function minimizes the boundary distance between the predicted and true boundaries, ensuring accurate delineation.

\bigskip

\textbf{Final Loss Function.} The final loss function combines the above three losses with appropriate weights:
\begin{equation}
L_{final} = \alpha \cdot L_{centroid} + \beta \cdot L_{local} + \gamma \cdot L_{boundary}
\label{eq:final_loss}
\end{equation}
where \( \alpha \), \( \beta \) and \( \gamma \) are hyperparameters that balance the contributions of each loss function.

\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}

\hspace{2em}This comprehensive loss function ensures that the model achieves high accuracy in both centroid localization, local region segmentation, and boundary delineation. By integrating these complementary loss components, the model becomes more robust to variations in dental morphology and arrangement, effectively handling cases with complex tooth geometry.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics}\label{subsec4_2}

\hspace{2em}To assess the segmentation performance, we employed the following metrics:

\bigskip

\hspace{2em} \textbf{Overall Accuracy (OA):} \hspace{1em} OA is a metric used to evaluate the overall accuracy of tooth segmentation \cite{zhang2021tsgcnet}. It is defined as the ratio of correctly predicted grids to the total number of grids, expressed mathematically as:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{OA}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{correct}}}{N_{\mathrm{total}}}
\end{equation}

where \( N_{correct} \) denotes the number of correctly predicted grids, and \( N_{total} \) represents the total number of grids. This metric reflects the model's general ability to segment accurately.

\bigskip

\hspace{2em} \textbf{Mean Intersection over Union (mIoU):} \hspace{1em}\( mIoU \)  is a measure of how well segmentation results match with ground truth annotations \cite{zhao2021two}. Its calculation formula is:

\begin{equation}
mIoU=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{i=0}^k\frac{p_{ii}}{\sum_{j=0}^kp_{ij}+\sum_{j=0}^kp_{ji}-p_{ii}}
\end{equation}

where \( k+1 \) represents the number of segmentation categories, \( p_{ij} \) denotes the probability that a grid predicted to belong to category i actually belongs to category j, and \( p_{ii} \) denotes the count of samples accurately predicted.

\bigskip

\hspace{2em} \textbf{Boundary Intersection over Union (B-IoU):} \hspace{1em}B-IoU is a metric specifically designed to evaluate the accuracy of tooth boundary segmentation, as referenced in \cite{cho2021weighted}. Its calculation formula is:

\begin{equation}
B\mathrm{-}IoU=\frac{\|A\cap B\|}{\|A\cup B\|}
\end{equation}

Here, \( A \) represents the predicted boundary points set, and \( B \) represents the ground truth boundary points set.

\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}
\hspace{2em}These formulas can help evaluate the performance of 3D tooth segmentation models, particularly in handling complex tooth arrangements and shape variations.

\subsection{Implementation Details}\label{subsec4_3}

\hspace{2em}Prior to training, all dental meshes undergo coordinate normalization: each mesh is centered at its centroid and scaled to fit within a unit sphere. This preprocessing ensures consistent scale across samples from different scanners and datasets, mitigating scale sensitivity. We adopted an end-to-end training approach to streamline the process and enable unified optimization. Specifically, the learning rate was initialized at $0.001$ with cosine annealing; the AdamW optimizer was employed with weight decay $0.01$; and a batch size of $8$ was selected to balance memory usage and stability. By combining optimized FPS sampling with this training strategy, PASegNet achieves practical efficiency for clinical 3D tooth segmentation.

\hspace{2em}For the loss function hyperparameters $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:final_loss}, we conducted a systematic grid search on the validation set, exploring weight combinations in the range $[0.1, 1.0]$ with a step size of $0.1$. Each configuration was evaluated based on validation mIoU and B-IoU metrics. The final weights ($\alpha=0.3$, $\beta=0.4$, $\gamma=0.3$) were selected to balance centroid localization, regional accuracy, and boundary precision, with the slightly higher $\beta$ reflecting the importance of local region segmentation accuracy.

\hspace{2em}Systematic timing experiments on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU demonstrate that PASegNet achieves an average inference time of 0.85 s per dental model (10,000 vertices) with only 4.2 GB GPU memory, enabling deployment on standard mid-range clinical workstations and real-time clinical applications. The architecture exhibits robust scalability across varying mesh resolutions (5,000 to 50,000 vertices), maintaining mIoU variance below 1.5\% with linear complexity relative to vertex count. This scalable AI infrastructure allows seamless integration into downstream clinical workflows, serving as a foundational segmentation engine for comprehensive digital dentistry solutions.

\hspace{2em}The ablation results presented in Table \ref{table_ablation_study}, together with the visualizations in Fig. \ref{fig:figure_boundary_comparison} and Fig. \ref{fig:figure_toothnumer_comparison}, clearly demonstrate the complementary advantages of the proposed dual-branch architecture and the incorporation of HKS features. The baseline DGCNN (single-branch) model achieves reasonable overall accuracy; however, it exhibits limited boundary precision, particularly in regions with complex geometry or adjacent teeth, as reflected by a B-IoU of only 73.67\%. Introducing HKS features in a single-branch configuration enhances local boundary delineation (B-IoU increases to 75.23\%), but this approach remains susceptible to missing or misclassified tooth regions due to the absence of global contextual information. In contrast, the dual-branch architecture, which effectively integrates global contextual features from the position-aware branch with local geometric details from the boundary-aware branch, achieves superior performance (B-IoU 82.56\%, mIoU 93.68\%).

\textbf{Effectiveness of different loss function.} To validate the effectiveness of different loss function combinations, we conducted ablation studies with four configurations: Centroid Only, Local Only, Boundary Only, and Final Loss, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:figure_train_curves}. Results demonstrated significant differentiation: Centroid Only showed stable but limited performance (mIoU \~70\%); Local Only exhibited rapid early convergence but overfitting after 120 epochs; Boundary Only encountered training difficulties with plateau phases yet achieved superior boundary IoU (87\%); Final Loss displayed complex dynamics with initial instability during the first 40 epochs due to multi-objective optimization, followed by synergistic advantages in the intermediate phase, ultimately achieving optimal performance, validating the efficacy of collaborative multi-loss optimization.

%% 4.4.3 Discussion
\subsubsection{Discussion}\label{subsubsec4_4_3}

\hspace{2em}The superior performance of PASegNet can be attributed to its innovative dual-branch architecture and attention-guided fusion module. By integrating the position-aware branch that captures global spatial relationships between teeth with the boundary-aware branch that focuses on local geometric details, our approach achieves a more comprehensive understanding of dental morphology. The attention-guided feature fusion dynamically balances global contextual information with fine-grained local features, enabling more accurate segmentation in complex scenarios such as abnormal tooth positions and varying morphologies. This ability to simultaneously process global position information and local boundary details allows PASegNet to effectively handle challenging cases where conventional single-branch methods typically fail. Additionally, our novel loss functions further enhance the model's capability to accurately locate tooth centroids and delineate boundaries, contributing to the overall robustness of the segmentation results.

\hspace{2em}Despite promising results, PASegNet has limitations. Performance decreases with extremely crowded or severely malposed teeth, as shown in Fig. \ref{fig:figure_constraints_examples}. This is because the manner of cascading in the G-Branch cannot adapt well to the positioning of supernumerary teeth. Moreover, excessive tooth substance loss can also affect the algorithm's ability to localize teeth. While our approach effectively utilizes geometric features, current evaluation requires validation across more diverse populations and scanning devices. Future research should incorporate anatomical prior knowledge through pretraining, integrate multimodal imaging data sources \cite{tian2023survey,kot2025evolution,pan2025mdgformer}, and explore model compression for clinical settings. These steps will close the research-to-application gap and enhance the algorithm’s clinical usability.

%% 5
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec5}

\hspace{2em}We presented PASegNet, a novel dual-branch framework for 3D tooth instance segmentation that effectively addresses challenges in dental morphologies through its position-aware and boundary-aware branches. The innovative attention-guided feature fusion mechanism and specialized loss functions further enhance the model's performance by optimizing centroid localization and boundary delineation simultaneously. Experimental results demonstrate that our approach outperforms state-of-the-art methods in segmentation accuracy and boundary detection precision. The clinical impact of PASegNet extends beyond technical improvements. By providing accurate and automated tooth segmentation, it can simplify orthodontic workflows, accelerate treatment planning, and enable more precise interventions. As a fundamental algorithm, PASegNet can serve as a basis for various dental design software applications, including automatic tooth arrangement, computer-aided diagnosis, and orthodontic treatment monitoring, positioning it to play a significant role in advancing automated analysis of 3D dental models for clinical applications.
