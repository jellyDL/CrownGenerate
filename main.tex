\begin{abstract}
Personalized dental crown design is a critical component of digital restorative workflows for structurally compromised teeth. This requires restoring patient-specific tooth morphology while ensuring marginal integrity and functional contacts. Conventional computer-aided design (CAD) relies on repeated template adjustments, making the process time-consuming and operator-dependent. To address these limitations, we propose a case-conditioned generative framework for posterior single crowns. Given full-arch maxillary and mandibular scans, a three-dimensional preparation margin line, and the FDI tooth designation, it directly generates a candidate crown mesh. The method employs a Flexible Dual Grid (FDG) representation with oriented signed-distance and surface-normal fields, while a sparse variational autoencoder (VAE) compresses high-resolution geometry into a compact latent space. Structure Flow and Feature Flow then sequentially generate crown occupancy and local surface features. Multiscale dental conditioning and surface supervision help preserve fine anatomical features and marginal continuity. Evaluation on an in-house dataset showed that the proposed method achieved the lowest \(L_1\) Chamfer distance among evaluated methods (\(0.126 \pm 0.027\,\mathrm{mm}\)) and improved geometric fidelity and spatial compatibility. Independent technician assessments further supported the usability of the generated crowns as CAD drafts for posterior single-crown restorations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}
\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}

\hspace{2em}Designing the external morphology of tooth-supported posterior single crowns is a key three-dimensional task in digital restorative dentistry. The crown must reproduce fine anatomical features, including cusps, ridges, grooves, and fossae, while maintaining a continuous contour along the manually delineated three-dimensional margin line and appropriate spatial relationships with adjacent teeth and the opposing dentition under static occlusion. Existing computer-aided design (CAD) and computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) systems typically require dental technicians to select a standard tooth template and iteratively position, deform, and sculpt it based on intraoral scans, making the process time-consuming and operator-dependent~\cite{Hosseinimanesh2025Thesis,Chen2024PreciseTooth,Wei2026MADCrowner}. These limitations in efficiency and reproducibility hinder consistent patient-specific customization and broader digital adoption in restorative dentistry.

\hspace{2em}Recent advances in artificial intelligence have enabled data-driven methods for automated dental crown design. Early approaches formulated occlusal-surface reconstruction as image generation from two-dimensional depth maps~\cite{Tian2022DentalRecNet}. Subsequent point-cloud, voxel-based, and template-deformation methods extended prediction to three-dimensional crown geometry while incorporating case-specific anatomy and boundary information~\cite{Hosseinimanesh2025PointToMesh,Yang2024DCrownFormer,Wei2025VBCD,Wei2026MADCrowner}. Together, these developments move crown generation beyond isolated shape prediction toward condition-aware reconstruction.

\hspace{2em}Meanwhile, general-purpose three-dimensional generative techniques have opened new avenues for high-resolution geometric synthesis. Flow matching learns generative transformations between distributions~\cite{lipman2023flow}, while structured latent representations separate sparse spatial structure from local geometric features~\cite{Xiang2025TRELLIS,xiang2026native}. Surface-neighborhood representations and sparse computation reduce the cost of high-resolution modeling compared with dense volumetric representations~\cite{He2025SparseFlex,Wu2025Direct3DS2}. Image-conditioned methods support the reconstruction and synthesis of three-dimensional assets from visual inputs~\cite{Tochilkin2024TripoSR,Zhao2025Hunyuan3D20,Hunyuan3DTeam2025Hunyuan3D21}. These approaches provide strategies for compressing geometric information, separating global structure from local detail, and conditioning generation on external observations.

\hspace{2em}Despite these advances, existing methods still struggle to preserve anatomy, margin geometry, and spatial compatibility. Projection-based methods lose occluded and axial-surface geometry, whereas point-based and volumetric methods face a trade-off between surface resolution and computational cost; template-deformation methods do not jointly enforce local anatomy and interarch compatibility. General-purpose generators remain insufficiently adapted to dental constraints. To address these limitations, the proposed framework uses a sparse Flexible Dual Grid (FDG) representation to encode signed distances and oriented normals, avoiding dense volumetric computation. It separately predicts global support and synthesizes local features, using multiscale dental conditioning and dual-level supervision to preserve crown morphology and spatial relationships.

\hspace{2em}The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

\hspace{1em}• Introduce an FDG representation augmented with truncated signed distances and oriented surface normals in high-resolution sparse cells. A sparse variational autoencoder subsequently compresses this representation into a compact latent space.

\hspace{1em}• Develop a two-stage conditional flow-matching framework comprising Structure Flow and Feature Flow, which model sparse occupancy and local geometric features, respectively. Dual-level supervision in the latent space and on decoded surfaces preserves geometric details and surface continuity.

\hspace{1em}• Design multiscale dental conditioning that integrates the maxillary and mandibular dentitions, the margin line, and the FDI tooth designation at the global, voxel-aligned, and coordinate-neighborhood levels, thereby coordinating tooth-position semantics, margin geometry, and dentition-level spatial relationships.

\hspace{1em}• Validate the method through geometric evaluations and independent technician assessments on an in-house dataset. Compared with the baselines, it achieves better geometric consistency and spatial compatibility, with favorable ratings for design quality and laboratory usability.

Section~\ref{sec4} describes the dataset, preprocessing, evaluation metrics, implementation details, experimental results, and discussion. Section~\ref{sec5} concludes the paper and discusses directions for future work.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec2}

\hspace{2em}This review covers related work, including data-driven dental crown generation, high-resolution three-dimensional geometry modeling, and dental spatial conditioning and evaluation, with emphasis on limitations in geometric representation, boundary enforcement, and candidate-mesh generation.

%% 2.1
\subsection{Data-Driven Dental Crown Morphology Generation}\label{subsec2_1}

\hspace{2em}Previous learning-based methods often formulate crown design as two-dimensional occlusal-surface reconstruction. DentalRecNet uses occlusal depth projections and dual discriminators to reconstruct defects in mandibular first molars~\cite{Tian2022DentalRecNet}. However, this representation loses geometric information from axial surfaces, cervical margins, and occluded regions, while image-space metrics cannot adequately characterize the crown's three-dimensional relationship with the surrounding dentition.

\hspace{2em}Among these approaches, point-cloud completion and point-to-mesh methods predict crown points and surface normals from preparation and antagonist inputs, followed by differentiable surface reconstruction to obtain the final mesh~\cite{Hosseinimanesh2025PointToMesh,Yang2024DCrownFormer,Yang2025DCrownFormerPlus}. Voxel-based methods incorporate curvature, margin, and FDI tooth-position information into volumetric crown generation~\cite{Wei2025VBCD}. Template-deformation methods combine cervical-margin information with multiscale anatomical conditioning and coarse-to-fine deformation of an initial tooth template~\cite{Wei2026MADCrowner}. These approaches demonstrate the value of dental context and boundary information; however, they remain subject to trade-offs involving sampling resolution, surface reconstruction, template dependence, local anatomical detail, and computational cost.

\hspace{2em}Implicit-field and diffusion models have further extended dental shape completion. ToothDIT constructs individualized implicit templates from complete anterior-tooth morphology, whereas ToothCraft performs conditional diffusion over signed-distance-function voxels~\cite{Chen2024PreciseTooth,Pukanec2026ToothCraft}. Several other studies investigate point-cloud completion and generative restoration for dental crowns~\cite{Ying2026Tooth3dNet,Shu2026AIDrivenCrown,Zhao2026ToothGAN,Tian2022DCPRGAN,Chanintonsongkhla2025PointFlow,Bae2026CrownGen}. Some of these methods are evaluated with anterior-tooth templates or synthetic data, which may limit their direct applicability to posterior crown design with prepared abutments and surrounding dental constraints.

%% 2.2
\subsection{High-Resolution Three-Dimensional Geometry Generation}\label{subsec2_2}

\hspace{2em}High-resolution three-dimensional generation requires a balance among representational capacity, topological flexibility, and computational cost. Dense signed-distance-field (SDF) and occupancy representations can express complex shapes, but their storage and computational demands scale cubically with resolution. Marching Cubes extracts a surface from these fields but does not learn mesh connectivity directly~\cite{Lorensen1987MarchingCubes}. Sparse generative representations address this cost by separating global structure from local geometric features and restricting computation to relevant spatial neighborhoods~\cite{Xiang2025TRELLIS,xiang2026native,li2026pixal3d,He2025SparseFlex,Wu2025Direct3DS2}. These methods provide transferable strategies for high-resolution representation and computationally efficient geometric generation; however, they do not explicitly model dental conditions or preparation-margin boundaries.

\hspace{2em}Explicit mesh-generation methods directly represent geometry and connectivity, providing an alternative paradigm for mesh synthesis. MeshFlow encodes continuous vertex positions and discrete connectivity relations in a latent space and generates them using rectified flow~\cite{Li_2026_CVPR}. LATO uses topology-preserving latents for mesh flow matching~\cite{Zhao2026LATO}, whereas FACE uses face-level autoregressive decoding~\cite{Wang2026FACE}. Shape As Points reconstructs surfaces from oriented points using a differentiable Poisson solver~\cite{Peng2021ShapeAsPoints}. Other approaches use signed-distance fields and orientation-aware information, such as surface normals, for geometric supervision~\cite{li2025triposg,Ye2025Hi3DGen}. These studies indicate that oriented surface information is an important complement to structural representations for capturing high-frequency geometry.

%% 2.3
\subsection{Dental Spatial Conditioning and Evaluation}\label{subsec2_3}

\hspace{2em}Dental conditions serve distinct roles in crown generation. The three-dimensional preparation-margin line provides a boundary reference for the cervical contour; adjacent teeth and the opposing dentition provide proximal and static geometric proximity constraints, respectively; and the FDI tooth designation supplies tooth-type and arch-related morphological priors. Previous methods introduce selected conditions at the input, loss, or post-processing stages~\cite{Wei2026MADCrowner,Yang2025DCrownFormerPlus,Wei2025VBCD}. However, existing studies do not consistently integrate tooth-position semantics, dentition-level spatial correspondences, and margin-neighborhood geometry within a unified multiscale framework.

\hspace{2em}Crown-design evaluation should jointly assess geometric fidelity, dental spatial compatibility, and laboratory usability. Differences in distance definitions, coordinate normalization, and numerical scaling can make direct comparisons across studies misleading~\cite{Yang2024DCrownFormer,Hosseinimanesh2025PointToMesh,Pukanec2026ToothCraft}. This study therefore combines surface-distance, margin, and static geometric-proximity metrics with technician assessments. These complementary measures evaluate overall geometry, local boundary fidelity, spatial relationships, and required modifications. The evaluation is restricted to CAD drafts of external crown morphology and does not establish internal fit, dynamic occlusal performance, or patient outcomes.

\hspace{2em}Collectively, the reviewed studies indicate three unresolved requirements for posterior crown generation: a representation that preserves high-resolution local geometry, including partially occluded surfaces; a conditioning mechanism that couples preparation-margin, tooth-position, and dentition-level spatial context; and an evaluation protocol that distinguishes surface fidelity, spatial compatibility, and practical usability. Sections~\ref{sec3} and~\ref{sec4} describe the proposed framework and its evaluation, respectively.

\section{Method}\label{sec3}

%% 3.1
\subsection{Overview}\label{subsec3_1}

\hspace{2em}The proposed framework generates CAD draft meshes of external crown morphology. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_overall_architecture}, it takes registered maxillary and mandibular scans, a three-dimensional preparation-margin line, and the FDI tooth designation as input, combining oriented signed-FDG representation learning, multiscale dental conditioning, and two-stage conditional flow matching. Panel (a) shows independent encoders that extract dental conditions for both generation stages; panel (b) shows a sparse variational autoencoder (VAE) that learns a representation of active support and local features; and panel (c) shows the sequential flow-matching stages, frozen VAE decoding, and direct FDG mesh extraction. The following subsections describe representation learning, dental conditioning, two-stage generation, and training objectives.

%% 3.2
\subsection{Sparse Geometric Encoding}\label{subsec3_2}

%% 3.2.1
\subsubsection{Oriented Signed-FDG Representation}\label{subsubsec3_2_1}

\hspace{2em}The FDG stores geometric information only in active cells within the crown surface neighborhood, thereby avoiding construction of a dense high-resolution voxel grid~\cite{xiang2026native}. For an active cell \(j\), let \(c_j\) denote its integer grid coordinate and \(x_j\) its center in the aligned physical domain. Standard FDG records the local dual-grid vertex offset \(v_j\in[0,1]^3\) and three axis-aligned intersection indicators \(b_j\in\{0,1\}^3\). These attributes encode geometry and connectivity but lack explicit signed-distance and normal cues, potentially hindering surface-side discrimination, orientation learning, and fine-detail reconstruction.

\hspace{2em}We therefore augment the FDG attributes with a truncated signed distance and an oriented normal. Before nearest-surface queries, reference meshes are cleaned and consistently oriented. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_oriented_signed_fdg_detail}, let \(p_j\) be the closest point on the reference surface to \(x_j\), \(n_j^f\) the unit normal of the closest face, and \(n_j\) the normalized, barycentrically interpolated vertex normal at \(p_j\), set to zero when the interpolated normal is degenerate. With \(\Delta\) denoting the physical grid spacing and \(\tau\) the dimensionless truncation bound, the truncated signed distance is defined as:
\begin{equation}
d_j=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
\operatorname{sign}\!\left[(x_j-p_j)^\top n_j^f\right]
\frac{\|x_j-p_j\|_2}{\Delta},-\tau,\tau
\right),\label{eq:oriented_signed_distance}
\end{equation}
where \(d_j\) is expressed in grid-spacing units, \(\|\cdot\|_2\) is the Euclidean norm, and \((\cdot)^\top\) denotes transpose. The sign function determines the local surface side from the nearest-face normal, and the clipping operator restricts the normalized distance to \([-\tau,\tau]\).

\hspace{2em}For each active cell, we concatenate the vertex offset, intersection indicators, truncated signed distance, and oriented normal into a local feature vector:
\begin{equation}
g_j=[v_j-0.5,\ b_j-0.5,\ d_j,\ n_j]\in\mathbb{R}^{10},
\label{eq:oriented_signed_feature}
\end{equation}
where subtracting \(0.5\) centers the offset and intersection channels. These attributes encode local geometry and surface orientation within the sparse neighborhood. Together with the active cell coordinates \(c_j\), they form the oriented signed-FDG representation used as input to the sparse geometric VAE.

%% 3.2.2
\subsubsection{Sparse VAE for Latent Compression}\label{subsubsec3_2_2}

\hspace{2em}The sparse geometric VAE compresses the high-resolution oriented signed-FDG representation into a latent space for generative modeling. Its multilevel encoder progressively reduces the active support, while the symmetric decoder reconstructs vertex offsets, axial-intersection logits, truncated signed distances, and normalized normals at the original resolution. At the bottleneck, \(O\) denotes the active latent-grid support and \(F\) the local feature field defined on that support; these components define the sparse latent \(Z=(O,F)\). Structure Flow predicts the support, and Feature Flow generates features on the predicted support. The decoder also predicts quadrilateral triangulation weights for differentiable FDG mesh extraction. During generation, the flow-predicted latent is decoded by the frozen VAE decoder to recover the crown geometry.

%% 3.3
\subsection{Multiscale Dental Conditions}\label{subsec3_3}

\hspace{2em}This section describes the multiscale dental conditions used by the two generation stages. After alignment and sampling, the maxillary, mandibular, and margin point sets are processed by independent condition encoders for Structure Flow and Feature Flow, indexed by \(d\in\{S,F\}\). Thus, \(G_S\) and \(V_S\) denote the global and voxel-aligned conditions for Structure Flow, while \(G_F\) and \(V_F\) denote those for Feature Flow. Maxillary and mandibular points use positions and surface normals, while margin points use positions only. Source-specific point-wise multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) with learned source embeddings encode each point set. The FDI code identifies the tooth quadrant and position; its embedding is added to the global condition \(G_d\). These global, voxel-aligned, and neighborhood conditions provide complementary case-level, spatial, and local context.

\bigskip

\textbf{Global condition $G_d$.} Features from each point set are aggregated using mean and max pooling, concatenated, and projected into a case-level vector. The FDI code, which specifies the tooth quadrant and position, is embedded and added to this vector to form $G_d$, providing overall dental context and tooth identity.

\bigskip

\textbf{Voxel-aligned condition \(V_d\).} Point features are assigned to a voxel grid according to their coordinates, aggregated within each cell, and refined by local convolutions. The resulting multiscale features provide spatially aligned dental context to the flow stages, complementing the global condition.

\bigskip

\textbf{Neighborhood condition \(N(\hat{O})\).} For each query coordinate \(q\), the Feature Flow encoder retrieves nearby points from the maxillary, mandibular, and margin point sets. An MLP encodes their relative positions, available normals, and distances into a local descriptor. At inference, descriptors are computed at the generated support coordinates and collected as \(N(\hat{O})\), providing local dental context to Feature Flow.

%% 3.4
\subsection{Two-Stage Conditional Flow Matching}\label{subsec3_4}

\hspace{2em}The two-stage generation architecture shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_feature_condition_detail} decomposes crown generation into support prediction and local feature generation. Structure Flow predicts the active latent-grid support \(\hat O\), and Feature Flow generates the local feature field \(\hat F\) on this support. Here, \(O\) and \(\hat O\) denote the reference and predicted active supports, respectively. The stages use independent condition encoders and velocity networks while sharing the flow-matching probability path defined in Sec.~\ref{subsec3_5}.

%% 3.4.1
\subsubsection{Structure Flow}\label{subsec3_4_1}

\hspace{2em}Structure Flow generates the sparse support for subsequent feature generation (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_feature_condition_detail}, top). The multiscale dental encoder maps the maxillary, mandibular, and margin inputs to global and spatial features, with the FDI embedding incorporated into the global condition. These features reach the Structure Diffusion Transformer (DiT)~\cite{peebles2023scalable} through two pathways. The projected global condition and the coarse voxel tokens provide case-level and spatial context for dental cross-attention. In parallel, spatial features are pooled onto the latent grid and linearly projected for aligned injection into each DiT block.

\hspace{2em}The noisy structural latent \(x_t^S\) is flattened and projected into tokens. Structure DiT processes these tokens through time-modulated self-attention, dental cross-attention, and feed-forward layers. Within each block, aligned features are added to the cross-attention residual. The output projection maps the updated tokens back to the latent grid to predict the velocity field. Starting from Gaussian noise, the ODE solver integrates this field to obtain \(\hat x_O^S\). Here \(x_t^S\) denotes the noisy structural latent at flow time \(t\), and \(\hat x_O^S\) denotes the denoised structural latent obtained after integration. After denormalization, the frozen structure decoder~\cite{Xiang2025TRELLIS,xiang2026native} converts the latent into occupancy scores. Occupancy selection produces the active support \(\hat O\), which defines the coordinates for Feature Flow.

%% 3.4.2
\subsubsection{Feature Flow}\label{subsec3_4_2}

\hspace{2em}Feature Flow generates local latent features on the active support through condition fusion, Feature DiT, and ODE integration (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_feature_condition_detail}, bottom). It operates on the active support \(S\) at coordinates \(q\in S\), with \(S=O\) during training and \(S=\hat O\) during inference. Noisy features \(x_t^F(q)\) first pass through a residual input adapter. In the residual-injection branch, linear maps project the global condition and the voxel features read at \(q\). In the local-modulation branch, MLPs encode dental neighborhood geometry and structural context from query positions and axial occupancy. Both branches contribute additively to the adapted features, forming the fused tokens that combine dental and structural context at the summation node.

\hspace{2em}The fused tokens retain their active coordinates and enter Feature DiT. Sparse self-attention exchanges information within the support, while dental cross-attention accesses the global and coarse voxel tokens from the feature condition encoder. Time modulation conditions processing on the current flow time. Feature DiT and its output adapter predict a velocity at each active coordinate. Starting from Gaussian noise, the ODE solver integrates these velocities with fixed support. Consequently, omitted cells cannot be recovered, while falsely activated cells may introduce spurious surface elements. The resulting features are denormalized to obtain \(\hat F\). At inference, they are paired with \(\hat O\) to form \(\hat Z=(\hat O,\hat F)\) for frozen VAE decoding. Here \(x_t^F(q)\) denotes the noisy feature latent at coordinate \(q\) and time \(t\), \(\hat F\) denotes the generated local feature field, and \(\hat Z\) denotes the predicted support--feature representation.

%% 3.5
\subsection{Training Objectives}\label{subsec3_5}

\hspace{2em}The sparse geometric VAE, Structure Flow, and Feature Flow are optimized sequentially, with stage-specific objectives formulated as follows.

%% 3.5.1
\subsubsection{VAE Reconstruction and Geometric Regularization}\label{subsubsec3_5_1}

\hspace{2em}The VAE reconstructs oriented signed-FDG attributes using an objective regularized by Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence to preserve local geometry and surface orientation:
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{VAE}} = L_{\mathrm{attr}} + \lambda L_{\mathrm{KL}}
+ L_{\mathrm{surf}} + L_{\mathrm{smooth}} + L_{\mathrm{cons}},
\label{eq:vae_training_objective}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ weights latent regularization. $L_{\mathrm{attr}}$ supervises geometry and connectivity via vertex-offset mean squared error (MSE), intersection and subdivision binary cross-entropy (BCE), and quadrilateral-midpoint error against diagonal midpoints. $L_{\mathrm{KL}}$ aligns the latent posterior with a standard normal prior~\cite{Kingma2013AutoEncoding}. $L_{\mathrm{surf}}$ supervises distances and normals via Smooth-L1~\cite{Girshick2015FastRCNN} and $1-\hat n^{\top}n$ for unit normals $\hat n,n$. $L_{\mathrm{smooth}}$ applies the same robust penalty to axial-difference and Laplacian errors in vertex, distance, and normal fields. $L_{\mathrm{cons}}$ penalizes predicted--reference residual differences between distance and normal fields:
\begin{equation}
r_{ij}=d_i-d_j-\tfrac12(n_i+n_j)^{\top}(c_i-c_j),
\label{eq:distance_normal_residual}
\end{equation}
where $i,j$ index neighboring active cells and $c_i,d_i,n_i$ denote grid coordinates, grid-spacing-unit signed distances, and normalized normals. It constrains local distance variation relative to surface orientation. Detail reconstruction combines robust vertex/distance errors with normal cosine errors. Reference-normal-variation weights highlight anatomical detail in the neighborhood, residual, and reconstruction terms. Settings are given in Sec.~\ref{subsec4_3}.

%% 3.5.2
\subsubsection{Structure Flow and Feature Flow Objectives}\label{subsec3_5_2}

\hspace{2em}Both stages learn the velocity fields used by the ODE solvers through the flow-matching path~\cite{lipman2023flow}:
\begin{equation}
x_t=(1-t)x_0+[\sigma+(1-\sigma)t]\epsilon,
\qquad u_t=(1-\sigma)\epsilon-x_0,
\label{eq:flow_training_path}
\end{equation}
where \(x_0\) and \(x_t\) denote the target and noisy latents, respectively; \(u_t\) is the target velocity; \(t\in[0,1]\) is the flow time; and \(\sigma\) is the residual noise scale. Here, \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) denotes standard Gaussian noise.

\hspace{2em}Structure Flow minimizes velocity MSE in the structural latent space:
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{Structure}}
=\mathbb{E}_{x_0^S,t,\epsilon}
\left[
\operatorname{MSE}\!\left(
v_{\theta_S}(x_t^S,t;G_S,V_S),u_t^S
\right)
\right],
\label{eq:structure_training_objective}
\end{equation}
where $v_{\theta_S}$ is the Structure DiT velocity field with parameters $\theta_S$, conditioned on the global and voxel-aligned features $G_S,V_S$. The target $x_0^S$, the noisy latent $x_t^S$, and the target velocity $u_t^S$ follow Eq.~\ref{eq:flow_training_path}, and MSE averages squared errors over latent entries. At inference, the ODE solver integrates the predicted field; after denormalization, the frozen decoder and occupancy selection yield the active support $\hat O$.


\hspace{2em}Feature Flow minimizes a flow-matching (FM) loss on the reference support with latent and decoded supervision:
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{Feature}}=L_{\mathrm{FM}}+\lambda_1L_{\mathrm{rec}}
+\lambda_2L_{\mathrm{grad}}+\lambda_3L_{\mathrm{dec}}+\lambda_4L_{\mathrm{end}},
\label{eq:feature_training_objective}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_4$ weight the auxiliary losses. $L_{\mathrm{FM}}$ applies time-weighted MSE to $v$, giving $\hat x_0=(1-\sigma)x_t-[\sigma+(1-\sigma)t]v$. $L_{\mathrm{rec}}$ matches $\hat x_0$ to $x_0$, while $L_{\mathrm{grad}}$ penalizes their axial-neighbor differences; both use $1-t$-weighted MSE. Velocity and reconstruction errors also use normalized target-neighborhood weights. $L_{\mathrm{dec}}$ compares frozen-VAE decodings of denormalized $\hat x_0$ and $x_0$ on overlapping endpoint cells using $1-t$ weights and detail-reconstruction, neighborhood, and distance--normal penalties, as well as BCE losses on signs, intersections, and subdivision. $L_{\mathrm{end}}$ is the MSE between an Euler-rollout endpoint and $x_0$. Sparse errors are averaged per case and overall.

\section{Experiments and Discussion}\label{sec4}

%% 4.1
\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}\label{subsec4_1}

\hspace{2em}The dataset comprised 5,049 processed gypsum-model cases obtained from multiple dental prosthetic laboratories. Each case included maxillary and mandibular gypsum models, a cervical margin line, an FDI designation, and a technician-designed reference CAD mesh; no patient-identifying or physiological information was included. Eligible cases had at least four adjacent teeth, valid margin and FDI records, and a valid maxillomandibular occlusion. Cases were randomly divided at the case level and stratified by FDI position, jaw, and laterality into 4,292 training, 505 validation, and 252 test cases. The test set included only posterior single crowns.

\hspace{2em}Preprocessing involved input validation, spatial alignment, and sampling. After verifying input completeness, we defined a right-handed local coordinate system from the margin curve and the opposing dentition. The target region was normalized to a domain with a half-extent of \(12\,\mathrm{mm}\). Each jaw was sampled at 4,096 points with associated normals, and the margin curve was sampled at 1,024 points. Sampling was controlled by a fixed random seed and the case identifier. Truncated signed distances were computed on a flexible dual grid.

%% 4.2
\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}\label{subsec4_2}

\hspace{2em}To assess geometric consistency, predicted crown and reference CAD models were compared using identical outer-surface segmentation, area-weighted sampling, registration, and post-processing. Each mesh was sampled at \(50{,}000\) points in physical coordinates. Following the cited protocols~\cite{Hosseinimanesh2025PointToMesh,Yang2024DCrownFormer,Yang2025DCrownFormerPlus,Wei2025VBCD,Wei2026MADCrowner}, the prespecified primary endpoints were symmetric Chamfer distance \(L_1\) (\(\mathrm{CD}\text{-}L_1\)) and 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance (\(\mathrm{HD}_{95}\)). \(\mathrm{CD}\text{-}L_1\) was the mean bidirectional Euclidean nearest-neighbor distance; \(\mathrm{HD}_{95}\) was the larger directional 95th percentile. Lower values indicated closer surface agreement. The F-score at \(0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\) (\(\mathrm{F}\text{-score@}0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\)) was defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall within this tolerance; higher values indicated greater coverage. The margin-to-surface 95th-percentile distance (Margin P95) was the unsigned nearest-surface distance from sampled margin points to the generated external surface, reflecting cervical correspondence. Severe penetration was defined per case as a minimum signed distance below \(-0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\) between the generated crown and registered opposing dentition; the severe-penetration rate was the proportion of evaluable cases meeting this criterion, indicating static geometric proximity.

%% 4.3
\subsection{Implementation Details}\label{subsec4_3}

\hspace{2em}The oriented signed-FDG representation used a grid with resolution \(R=1024\) and truncation parameter \(\tau=3\). The five-level sparse VAE used channel widths of 48, 96, 192, 384, and 512, with two blocks per level. Four spatial-to-channel rearrangements produced 32-channel latents on a sparse \(64^3\) grid; each active location predicted 32-dimensional mean and log-variance vectors. Global conditions were projected to 512 dimensions. Voxel-aligned conditions used \(64^3\) and \(16^3\) grids; feature conditioning used four neighbors per source with 7-dimensional descriptors normalized by \(h=12\)~mm. Training was performed on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 GPU. The VAE and both flows were sequentially optimized with Adam using mixed precision, a weight decay of 0.01, learning rates of \(10^{-5}\)--\(10^{-4}\), and cosine decay after 1,000 warm-up steps.

\hspace{2em}For the two flow networks, the Transformer blocks used rotary embeddings and memory-efficient exact attention~\cite{Su2024RoFormer,Dao2022FlashAttention}. The sparse VAE remained frozen during Structure Flow training, and Feature Flow used rank-32  LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora}. Flow training used \(\sigma=10^{-5}\), auxiliary weights \((0.6,0.35,3.0,0.5)\), velocity weighting \((1+2t^4)/1.4\), decoded   supervision at \(t\leq0.75\), and four Euler endpoint steps. At inference, one candidate per case was generated with a fixed seed. Both flows used 50 steps    with the Heun and Euler solvers, respectively, with a classifier-free guidance scale of 3.0~\cite{Ho2022ClassifierFree}. The frozen VAE decoded latents into     oriented signed-FDG attributes for direct FDG mesh extraction. After mesh cleanup and largest-component retention, checks covered watertightness, consistent orientation, genus-0 topology, and geometric validity.

%% 4.4.3 Discussion
\subsubsection{Discussion}\label{subsubsec4_4_3}

\noindent\textbf{Interpretation of Results.}
This approach combines geometric precision with dental context to jointly address anatomical fidelity and spatial compatibility. Oriented signed-FDG encodes surface distance and orientation, while the sparse VAE supports high-resolution reconstruction through a compact latent representation. Structure Flow predicts global occupancy support, enabling Feature Flow to concentrate on anatomical details within active regions. Multiscale dental conditioning guides both stages using tooth identity, cervical-margin geometry, and the surrounding dentition, while decoded-surface supervision directly links latent learning to reconstruction accuracy. These complementary mechanisms provide a coherent explanation for the observed improvements in geometric accuracy and cervical-margin consistency, together with reduced antagonist penetration.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations and Scope of Application.}
Inadequate tooth preparation, incomplete dental scans, and atypical morphology pose risks to generation quality. Fig.~\ref{fig:false_cases} shows preserved global crown form but localized pit-and-fissure failures, including interrupted grooves, hole-like depressions, fragments, and spike-like protrusions, possibly because no explicit pit-and-fissure constraint is imposed. Direct FDG extraction may be sensitive to local geometry and connectivity inconsistencies, producing sliver faces or protrusions that persist after post-processing. Therefore, the generated crown should be treated as a CAD draft for external crown morphology and must be inspected and corrected before downstream use; the reference CAD should be regarded as a historical design instance rather than a unique ground truth. Future work should investigate controllable editing of localized crown details (e.g., cusps, ridges, grooves, and fossae), explicit constraints on pit-and-fissure continuity and local surface topology, and surface texture modeling beyond geometry alone, followed by independent laboratory validation, \textit{in vitro} measurements, and prospective workflow studies.

%% 5
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec5}

\hspace{2em}We present a case-conditioned framework for generating the external morphology of tooth-supported posterior single crowns. The oriented signed-FDG representation and a sparse VAE efficiently encode local geometry, signed distance, and oriented normals within the crown surface neighborhood, while two-stage conditional flow matching with Structure Flow and Feature Flow separately models global support and local anatomical detail. Global, voxel-aligned, and coordinate-neighborhood conditions further integrate maxillary and mandibular scans, a three-dimensional margin line, and FDI tooth-position information, allowing tooth-type priors, spatial arch relationships, and local boundary constraints to be incorporated during generation. Together with direct FDG mesh extraction, mesh cleanup, and engineering quality control, the framework establishes an end-to-end pathway from case inputs to candidate triangular meshes and provides a technical basis for high-resolution crown design suitable for subsequent CAD refinement. Future work will extend the framework to broader tooth positions, missing-tooth patterns, and restorative settings; enable controllable editing of local crown details; incorporate surface texture information in addition to geometry; incorporate dynamic occlusion, manufacturing constraints, and human-in-the-loop design information; and investigate its use for anterior crowns, implant-supported restorations, and multi-unit prostheses to strengthen integration with digital restorative workflows.
