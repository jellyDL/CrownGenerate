%% Abstract
\begin{abstract}
%% Text of abstract 
Personalized dental crown design is a critical component of digital restorative workflows for structurally compromised teeth. It requires reconstructing detailed anatomy while maintaining compatibility with the preparation margin, adjacent teeth, and opposing dentition. Conventional computer-aided design (CAD) relies on repeated template adjustment, making the process time-consuming and operator-dependent. To address this limitation, we propose a case-conditioned generative method for tooth-supported posterior single crowns. Given full-arch maxillary and mandibular scans, a three-dimensional preparation margin line, and the FDI tooth designation, the method directly generates an editable crown mesh. It employs a Flexible Dual Grid (FDG) representation with oriented signed-distance and surface-normal fields, while a sparse variational autoencoder compresses high-resolution geometry into a compact latent space. Structure Flow and Feature Flow sequentially generate crown occupancy and local surface features. Multiscale dental conditioning and surface supervision help preserve anatomical details, marginal continuity, and spatial relationships within the dentition. Evaluation on an in-house dataset showed that the proposed method achieved the lowest CD-$L_1$ error ($0.126 \pm 0.027$ mm), with improved geometric reconstruction and spatial compatibility. Independent technician assessments supported the usability of the generated crowns as CAD drafts for posterior single-crown restorations.
\end{abstract}

%% 1. Introduction
\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}
\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}

\hspace{2em}Designing the external morphology of tooth-supported posterior single crowns is a key three-dimensional task in digital restorative dentistry. The crown must reproduce fine anatomical features, including cusps, ridges, grooves, and fossae, while maintaining a continuous contour along the manually delineated three-dimensional margin line and appropriate spatial relationships with adjacent teeth and the opposing dentition under static occlusion. Existing computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM) systems typically require dental technicians to select a standard tooth template and iteratively position, deform, and sculpt it based on intraoral scans, making the process time-consuming and operator-dependent~\cite{Hosseinimanesh2025Thesis,Chen2024PreciseTooth,Wei2026MADCrowner}. These limitations in efficiency and reproducibility hinder consistent patient-specific customization and broader digital adoption in restorative dentistry.

\hspace{2em}Recent advances in artificial intelligence have enabled data-driven methods for automated dental crown design. Early approaches formulated occlusal-surface reconstruction as image generation from two-dimensional depth maps~\cite{Tian2022DentalRecNet}. Subsequent point-cloud completion and point-to-mesh approaches extended prediction to three-dimensional crown geometry, incorporating anatomical context, curvature supervision, and differentiable surface reconstruction~\cite{Hosseinimanesh2025PointToMesh,Yang2024DCrownFormer}. Voxel-based methods further incorporated curvature, margin, and tooth-position information~\cite{Wei2025VBCD}. Template-deformation approaches combined cervical-margin segmentation, multiscale anatomical conditioning, coarse-to-fine deformation, and margin-guided surface trimming~\cite{Wei2026MADCrowner}. Together, these developments extend crown generation beyond isolated shape prediction by incorporating dental context and boundary information.

\hspace{2em}Meanwhile, general-purpose three-dimensional generative techniques have opened new avenues for high-resolution geometric synthesis. Flow matching learns generative transformations between distributions~\cite{Lipman2023FlowMatching}, while structured latent representations separate sparse spatial structure from local geometric features~\cite{Xiang2025TRELLIS,Xiang2025TRELLIS2}. Surface-neighborhood representations and sparse computation reduce the cost of high-resolution modeling compared with dense volumetric representations~\cite{He2025SparseFlex,Wu2025Direct3DS2}. Image-conditioned methods support the reconstruction and synthesis of three-dimensional assets from visual inputs~\cite{Tochilkin2024TripoSR,Zhao2025Hunyuan3D20,Hunyuan3DTeam2025Hunyuan3D21}. These approaches provide strategies for compressing geometric information, separating global structure from local detail, and conditioning generation on external observations.

\hspace{2em}Despite these advances, existing methods struggle to preserve anatomy, margin geometry, and spatial compatibility. Projections lose occluded information; Point-based and volumetric methods are resolution-limited; template deformation needs initialization and boundary accuracy. Generating dental crowns that simultaneously satisfy all these constraints remains challenging. To address these limitations, we propose a case-conditioned framework for dental crown generation. It combines a Flexible Dual Grid (FDG), oriented signed-distance, surface-normal fields, and multiscale flow matching. The oriented signed-FDG captures high-resolution geometry while lowering computational cost. A sparse variational autoencoder compresses the representation while preserving anatomical details. Structure Flow generates occupancy, whereas Feature Flow predicts local geometry, separating global morphology from fine detail. Multiscale conditioning integrates tooth semantics, margin geometry, spatial correspondences, and opposing-dentition constraints. Latent and surface supervision improve consistency and reduce margin artifacts, yielding efficient, editable crown meshes.

\hspace{2em}The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

\hspace{1em}• Introduce a Flexible Dual Grid (FDG) representation augmented with truncated signed distances and oriented surface normals in high-resolution sparse cells. A sparse variational autoencoder subsequently compresses this representation into a compact latent space.

\hspace{1em}• Develop a two-stage conditional flow-matching framework comprising Structure Flow and Feature Flow, which model sparse occupancy and local geometric features, respectively. Dual-level supervision in the latent space and on decoded surfaces preserves geometric details and surface continuity.       

\hspace{1em}• Design a three-level conditioning scheme that integrates the maxillary and mandibular dentitions, the 3D margin line, and the FDI tooth designation at the global, voxel-aligned, and coordinate-neighborhood levels, thereby coordinating tooth-position semantics, margin geometry, and dentition-level spatial relationships.

\hspace{1em}• Validate the method through geometric evaluations and independent technician assessments on an in-house dataset. Compared with the baselines, it achieves better geometric consistency and spatial compatibility, with favorable ratings for design quality and laboratory usability.

% Accordingly, this study focuses on the generation of a case-adapted, editable crown mesh conditioned on registered full-arch maxillary and mandibular scans, a three-dimensional margin line, and the FDI tooth designation. The scope is limited to the external morphology of tooth-supported posterior single crowns and excludes intaglio-surface design, cement-space definition, manufacturing compensation, and fabrication-ready restoration generation.

%% 2 Related work
\section{Related work}\label{sec2}

\hspace{2em}This review covers related work, including data-driven dental crown generation, high-resolution 3D geometry modeling, and dental spatial conditioning and evaluation, with emphasis on limitations in geometric representation, boundary enforcement, and editable mesh generation.

%% 2.1
\subsection{Data-Driven Dental Crown Morphology Generation}\label{subsec2_1}

\hspace{2em}Previous learning-based methods often formulate crown design as two-dimensional occlusal-surface reconstruction. DentalRecNet uses occlusal depth projections and dual discriminators to reconstruct defects in mandibular first molars~\cite{Tian2022DentalRecNet}. However, this representation loses geometric information from axial surfaces, cervical margins, and occluded regions, while image-space metrics cannot adequately characterize the crown's three-dimensional relationship with the surrounding dentition.

\hspace{2em}Subsequently, Point-cloud, voxel-based, and template-deformation methods have extended crown prediction to three-dimensional geometry while incorporating case-specific conditions. Point-cloud completion and point-to-mesh methods predict crown points and surface normals from preparation and antagonist inputs, followed by differentiable surface reconstruction to obtain the final mesh~\cite{Hosseinimanesh2025PointToMesh,Yang2024DCrownFormer,Yang2025DCrownFormerPlus}. Voxel-based methods incorporate curvature, margin, and FDI tooth-position information into volumetric crown generation~\cite{Wei2025VBCD}. Template-deformation methods combine cervical-margin information with multiscale anatomical conditioning and coarse-to-fine deformation of an initial tooth template~\cite{Wei2026MADCrowner}. These approaches demonstrate the value of dental context and boundary information, but remain subject to trade-offs involving sampling resolution, surface reconstruction, template dependence, local anatomical detail, and computational cost.

\hspace{2em}Implicit-field and diffusion models have further extended dental shape completion. ToothDIT constructs individualized implicit templates from complete anterior-tooth morphology, whereas ToothCraft performs conditional diffusion over signed-distance-function voxels~\cite{Chen2024PreciseTooth,Pukanec2026ToothCraft}. Several other studies investigate point-cloud completion and generative restoration for dental crowns~\cite{Zhu2022ToothCR,Ying2026Tooth3dNet,Shu2026AIDrivenCrown,Zhao2026ToothGAN,Tian2022DCPRGAN,Chanintonsongkhla2025PointFlow,Bae2026CrownGen}. Some of these methods are evaluated with anterior-tooth templates or synthetic data, which may limit their direct applicability to posterior crown design with prepared abutments and surrounding dental constraints.

%% 2.2
\subsection{High-Resolution 3D Geometry Generation}\label{subsec2_2}

\hspace{2em}High-resolution three-dimensional generation requires a balance among representational capacity, topological flexibility, and computational cost. Dense signed-distance-field (SDF) and occupancy representations can express complex shapes, but their storage and computational demands scale cubically with resolution. Marching Cubes extracts a surface from these fields but does not learn mesh connectivity directly~\cite{Lorensen1987MarchingCubes}. Sparse generative representations address this cost by separating global structure from local geometric features and restricting computation to relevant spatial neighborhoods~\cite{Xiang2025TRELLIS,Xiang2025TRELLIS2,Li2026Pixal3D,He2025SparseFlex,Wu2025Direct3DS2}. These methods provide transferable strategies for high-resolution representation and computationally efficient geometric generation, but they do not explicitly model dental conditions or preparation-margin boundaries.

\hspace{2em}Explicit mesh-generation methods directly represent geometry and connectivity, providing an alternative paradigm for mesh synthesis. MeshFlow encodes continuous vertex positions and discrete connectivity relations in a latent space and generates them using rectified flow~\cite{Li2026MeshFlow}. LATO uses topology-preserving latents for mesh flow matching~\cite{Zhao2026LATO}, whereas FACE uses face-level autoregressive decoding~\cite{Wang2026FACE}. Shape As Points reconstructs surfaces from oriented points using a differentiable Poisson solver~\cite{Peng2021ShapeAsPoints}. Other approaches use signed-distance fields and orientation-aware information, such as surface normals, for geometric supervision~\cite{Li2025TripoSG,Ye2025Hi3DGen}. These studies indicate that oriented surface information is an important complement to structural representations for capturing high-frequency geometry.

%% 2.3
\subsection{Dental Spatial Conditioning and Evaluation}\label{subsec2_3}

\hspace{2em}Dental conditions serve distinct roles in crown generation. The three-dimensional preparation-margin line provides a boundary reference for the cervical contour; adjacent teeth and the opposing dentition provide proximal and static geometric proximity constraints, respectively; and the FDI tooth designation supplies tooth-type and arch-related morphological priors. Previous methods introduce selected conditions at the input, loss, or post-processing stages~\cite{Wei2026MADCrowner,Yang2025DCrownFormerPlus,Wei2025VBCD}. However, existing studies do not consistently integrate tooth-position semantics, dentition-level spatial correspondences, and margin-neighborhood geometry within a unified multiscale framework.

\hspace{2em}Crown-design evaluation should jointly assess geometric fidelity, dental spatial compatibility, and laboratory usability. Differences in distance definitions, coordinate normalization, and numerical scaling can make direct comparisons across studies misleading~\cite{Yang2024DCrownFormer,Hosseinimanesh2025PointToMesh,Pukanec2026ToothCraft}. This study therefore combines surface-distance, margin, and static geometric-proximity metrics with technician assessments. These complementary measures evaluate overall geometry, local boundary fidelity, spatial relationships, and required modifications. The evaluation is restricted to CAD drafts of external crown morphology and does not establish internal fit, dynamic occlusal performance, or patient outcomes.

\section{Method}\label{sec3}

%% 3.1
\subsection{Overview}\label{subsec3_1}

\hspace{2em}The proposed framework generates editable external crown morphology for tooth-supported posterior single crowns. It takes registered maxillary and mandibular scans, a three-dimensional preparation-margin line, and the FDI tooth designation as inputs, combining oriented signed-FDG representation learning, multiscale dental conditioning, and two-stage conditional flow matching. Reference CAD meshes provide supervision during training and are not required at inference.

\hspace{2em}Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_overall_architecture} summarizes the framework. Panel (a) extracts dental conditions using independent encoders for the two generation stages. Panel (b) learns a sparse VAE representation comprising active support and local features. In panel (c), Structure Flow predicts the active support, and Feature Flow generates local features at the predicted locations. Frozen VAE decoding produces oriented signed-FDG attributes, followed by direct FDG mesh extraction. The following subsections detail representation learning, dental conditioning, two-stage generation, and training objectives; reconstruction settings are provided in Sec.~\ref{subsec4_3}.

%% 3.2
\subsection{Oriented signed-FDG Representation and Sparse VAE}\label{subsec3_2}

%% 3.2.1
\subsubsection{Oriented signed-FDG Representation}\label{subsubsec3_2_1}

\hspace{2em}The Flexible Dual Grid (FDG) stores geometric information only in active cells within the crown surface neighborhood, thereby avoiding construction of a dense high-resolution voxel grid~\cite{Xiang2025TRELLIS2}. For a grid of resolution \(R\), let \(c_j\in\{0,\ldots,R-1\}^3\) denote an active cell coordinate and \(\xi_j=c_j+0.5\) its center. The corresponding local physical coordinate is \(x_j=2h(\xi_j/R-0.5)\). Standard FDG encodes the within-cell offset of the dual-grid vertex, \(v_j\in[0,1]^3\), and three axial intersection indicators, \(b_j\in\{0,1\}^3\). Its nominal spacing, \(\Delta=2h/R\), specifies the discretization resolution rather than scan or clinical measurement accuracy.

\hspace{2em}Standard FDG represents local geometry and connectivity but does not explicitly encode the two sides of a surface or its orientation. We therefore augment it with a truncated signed distance and an oriented normal. Before nearest-surface queries, we repair inconsistent mesh winding, reverse watertight meshes with negative signed volume, and remove degenerate triangles. Let \(p_j\) be the closest point on the reference surface to \(x_j\), \(n_j^f\) the unit normal of the closest triangle, and \(n_j\) the barycentrically interpolated and normalized vertex normal at \(p_j\), set to zero in degenerate cases. The oriented truncated signed distance and normal feature are defined as
\[
\begin{aligned}
d_j&=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
\operatorname{sign}\!\left[(x_j-p_j)^\top n_j^f\right]
\frac{\|x_j-p_j\|_2}{\Delta},-\tau,\tau
\right),\quad \tau=3,\\
g_j&=[v_j-0.5,\ b_j-0.5,\ d_j,\ n_j]\in\mathbb R^{10}.
\end{aligned}
\]
This representation jointly encodes local geometry, distance, and orientation within the sparse surface neighborhood. For watertight, consistently oriented meshes, the sign of \(d_j\) denotes global inside--outside status; for non-watertight meshes, it indicates only the local direction defined by the nearest surface normal. Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_oriented_signed_fdg_detail} illustrates the latent encoding of sparse active cells and their oriented signed attributes.

%% 3.2.2
\subsubsection{Sparse Geometric VAE}\label{subsubsec3_2_2}

\hspace{2em}The sparse geometric VAE compresses the high-resolution representation into a latent space suitable for generative modeling. Its five-level encoder has channel widths of 48, 96, 192, 384, and 512, with two encoding blocks per level. Four spatial-to-channel rearrangements reduce the active support at \(1024^3\) resolution to a 32-channel latent representation defined on a sparse \(64^3\) grid, where each active location predicts a 32-dimensional mean vector and log-variance vector. VAE training uses reparameterized sampling; the posterior mean is used for validation, free decoding, and Flow latent caching, whereas generation directly uses latents predicted by the Flow models. The symmetric decoder progressively predicts refinement masks and restores vertex offsets, axial-intersection logits, truncated signed distances, and normalized normals at the original resolution. It also predicts the quadrilateral triangulation weights required for differentiable FDG mesh extraction.

%% 3.3
\subsection{Multiscale Dental Conditions}\label{subsec3_3}

\hspace{2em}This section defines the multiscale dental conditions required by the two-stage generation. After alignment and sampling, the maxillary, mandibular, and margin point sets are fed into two conditional encoders with independent parameters corresponding to the Structure Flow and Feature Flow stages. In the following, $d\in\{S,F\}$ indexes the stage: the maxillary and mandibular points take both position and normal as input, whereas the margin points use positions only. Each of the three sources is first encoded by a source-specific point-wise multilayer perceptron (MLP), augmented with learnable source embeddings.

\bigskip

\textbf{Global condition $C_d^g$.} For each of the three sources, the features are separately subjected to mean pooling and max pooling, concatenated, and projected into a 512-dimensional vector. An FDI embedding is then added to produce the case-level condition $C_d^g$.

\bigskip

\textbf{Voxel-aligned condition $C_{d,64}^v$.} Point features are scattered onto a $64^3$ grid according to their local coordinates, averaged within each voxel, and refined by $3\times3\times3$ convolutions to yield $C_{d,64}^v$. Its downsampled counterpart is denoted $C_{d,16}^v$.

\bigskip

\textbf{Coordinate-level neighborhood condition $C_F^n(q)$.} For a query coordinate $q$, the Feature encoder retrieves four nearest neighbors from the maxillary, mandibular, and margin point sets, respectively. Each neighbor is represented by a 7-dimensional descriptor composed of relative coordinates, source normal, and distance; both the relative coordinates and the distance are normalized by $h=12$~mm, and the 3-D normal for margin points is set to zero. The resulting $3\times4\times7$ descriptor is projected by an MLP into $C_F^n(q)$. During inference, $\{C_F^n(q)\mid q\in\hat O\}$ corresponds to $N(\hat O)$ in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_overall_architecture}.

%% 3.4
\subsection{Two-Stage Conditional Flow Matching}\label{subsec3_4}

\hspace{2em}Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_feature_condition_detail} shows the two-stage generation architecture. Structure Flow predicts the active support \(\hat O\), which specifies where Feature Flow generates local latent features. The two stages use independent condition encoders and velocity networks while sharing the flow-matching probability path defined in Sec.~\ref{subsec3_5}.

%% 3.4.1
\subsubsection{Structure Flow}\label{subsec3_4_1}

\hspace{2em}Structure Flow generates the sparse support for subsequent feature generation (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_feature_condition_detail}, top). The multiscale dental encoder maps maxillary, mandibular, and margin inputs to global and spatial features, with the FDI embedding incorporated into the global condition. These features reach Structure DiT through two pathways. The projected global condition and coarse voxel tokens provide the context for dental cross-attention. In parallel, spatial features are pooled onto the latent grid and linearly projected for aligned injection into each DiT block.

\hspace{2em}The noisy structural latent \(x_t^S\) is flattened and projected into tokens. Structure DiT processes these tokens through time-modulated self-attention, dental cross-attention, and feed-forward layers. Within each block, aligned features are added to the cross-attention residual. The output projection maps the updated tokens back to the latent grid to predict the velocity field. Starting from Gaussian noise, the ODE solver integrates this field to obtain \(\hat x_0^S\). After denormalization, the frozen structure decoder~\cite{Xiang2025TRELLIS,Xiang2025TRELLIS2} converts the latent into occupancy scores. Occupancy selection produces the active support \(\hat O\), which defines the coordinates for Feature Flow.

%% 3.4.2
\subsubsection{Feature Flow}\label{subsec3_4_2}

\hspace{2em}Feature Flow generates local latent features on the active support through condition fusion, Feature DiT, and ODE integration (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_feature_condition_detail}, bottom). It operates at coordinates \(q\in S\), with \(S=O\) during training and \(S=\hat O\) during inference. Noisy features \(x_t^F(q)\) first pass through a residual input adapter. In the residual-injection branch, linear maps project the global condition and voxel features read at \(q\). In the local-modulation branch, MLPs encode dental neighborhood geometry and structural context from query positions and axial occupancy. Both branches contribute additively to the adapted features, forming the fused tokens shown at the summation node.

\hspace{2em}The support is held fixed throughout Feature Flow integration. Consequently, a false-negative cell in \(\hat O\) is unavailable to Feature Flow and cannot be recovered at this stage, whereas a false-positive cell receives a generated feature and may introduce an isolated or spurious surface element. This support dependency is the source of the training--inference mismatch and is evaluated explicitly in Sec.~\ref{subsec4_2}; oracle-support results, when available, are diagnostic only and are not part of the deployable pipeline.

\hspace{2em}The fused tokens retain their active coordinates and enter Feature DiT. Sparse self-attention exchanges information within the support, while dental cross-attention accesses global and coarse voxel tokens from the Feature condition encoder. Time modulation conditions this processing on the current flow time. Feature DiT and its output adapter predict a velocity for each active coordinate. Starting from Gaussian noise, the ODE solver integrates these velocities while keeping the support fixed. The resulting features are denormalized to obtain \(\hat F\). At inference, they are paired with \(\hat O\) to form \(\hat Z=(\hat O,\hat F)\) for frozen VAE decoding.

%% 3.5
\subsection{Training Objectives}\label{subsec3_5}

\hspace{2em}The sparse geometric VAE, Structure Flow, and Feature Flow are optimized sequentially, with stage-specific objectives formulated as follows.

%% 3.5.1
\subsubsection{VAE Reconstruction and Geometric Regularization}\label{subsubsec3_5_1}

\hspace{2em}The VAE reconstructs oriented signed-FDG attributes by minimizing
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{VAE}} = L_{\mathrm{attr}} + \lambda L_{\mathrm{KL}}
+ L_{\mathrm{surf}} + L_{\mathrm{smooth}} + L_{\mathrm{cons}},
\label{eq:vae_training_objective}
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda\) weights latent regularization and composite terms include their component weights. \(L_{\mathrm{attr}}\) combines vertex-offset MSE, intersection and subdivision BCE, and quadrilateral-midpoint error measured against reference diagonal midpoints. \(L_{\mathrm{KL}}\) averages \(\tfrac12(\mu^2+e^{\ell}-\ell-1)\) over latent entries to regularize the posterior toward a standard normal prior~\cite{Kingma2013AutoEncoding}; \(\mu,\ell\) denote posterior mean and clipped log-variance.

\hspace{2em}\(L_{\mathrm{surf}}\) supervises signed distances and normal alignment. Midpoint and distance errors use Smooth-L1~\cite{Girshick2015FastRCNN}, while normal alignment uses \(1-\hat n^{\top}n\) for predicted and reference unit normals \(\hat n,n\). To constrain local geometry, \(L_{\mathrm{smooth}}\) applies the same robust penalty to axial-difference and Laplacian errors in vertex, distance, and normal fields; Laplacians average valid center-minus-neighbor differences.

\hspace{2em}The consistency term \(L_{\mathrm{cons}}\) applies this penalty to differences between predicted and reference distance--normal residuals:
\begin{equation}
r_{ij}=d_i-d_j-\tfrac12(n_i+n_j)^{\top}(c_i-c_j),
\label{eq:distance_normal_residual}
\end{equation}
where \(i,j\) index neighboring active cells and \(c_i,d_i,n_i\) denote grid coordinates, signed distance in grid-spacing units, and normalized normals. Detail reconstruction averages the same robust errors for vertices and distances with normal cosine errors. Reference-normal-variation weights emphasize anatomical detail in neighborhood, residual, and reconstruction terms. Training and inference settings are provided in Sec.~\ref{subsec4_3}.

%% 3.5.2
\subsubsection{Structure Flow and Feature Flow Objectives}\label{subsec3_5_2}

\hspace{2em}Both stages use the flow-matching path~\cite{Lipman2023FlowMatching}
\begin{equation}
x_t=(1-t)x_0+[\sigma+(1-\sigma)t]\epsilon,
\qquad u_t=(1-\sigma)\epsilon-x_0,
\label{eq:flow_training_path}
\end{equation}
where \(x_0,x_t\) are target and noisy latents, \(u_t\) is target velocity, \(t\in[0,1]\) is time, and \(\sigma\) is the residual noise scale. Noise follows \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\). Structure Flow minimizes velocity mean squared error (MSE).

\begin{samepage}
\hspace{2em}Feature Flow minimizes
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{Feature}}=L_{\mathrm{FM}}+\lambda_1L_{\mathrm{rec}}
+\lambda_2L_{\mathrm{grad}}+\lambda_3L_{\mathrm{dec}}+\lambda_4L_{\mathrm{end}},
\label{eq:feature_training_objective}
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_4\) balance the auxiliary losses. \(L_{\mathrm{FM}}\) is time-weighted velocity MSE. Predicted velocity \(v\) yields \(\hat x_0=(1-\sigma)x_t-[\sigma+(1-\sigma)t]v\). \(L_{\mathrm{rec}}\) and \(L_{\mathrm{grad}}\) compare \(\hat x_0\) with \(x_0\) using MSE in latent values and axial-neighbor differences, respectively, weighted by \(1-t\). Velocity and reconstruction errors additionally use normalized target-neighborhood weights.
\end{samepage}

\hspace{2em}\(L_{\mathrm{dec}}\) extends geometric supervision to frozen-VAE decodings of denormalized \(\hat x_0\) and \(x_0\), reusing the detail-reconstruction, neighborhood, and distance--normal consistency penalties above, together with BCE for signs, intersections, and subdivision. Surface comparisons use overlapping cells at near-data times, weighted by the selected cases' mean \(1-t\). \(L_{\mathrm{end}}\) is latent MSE between an Euler-rollout endpoint and \(x_0\). Sparse errors are averaged within each case, then across cases.

\section{Experimental and Discussion}\label{sec4}

%% 4.1 
\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}\label{subsec4_1}

\hspace{2em}The dataset comprised 5,049 processed gypsum-model cases obtained from multiple dental prosthetic laboratories. Each case included maxillary and mandibular gypsum models, a cervical margin line, an FDI designation, and a technician-designed reference CAD mesh, with no patient-identifying or physiological information. Eligible cases had at least four adjacent teeth, valid margin and FDI records, and a valid maxillomandibular occlusion. Cases were randomly split at the case level, stratified by FDI position, jaw, and laterality, into 4,292 training, 505 validation, and 252 test cases. The test set included only posterior single crowns.

\hspace{2em}Preprocessing involved input validation, spatial alignment, and sampling. After verifying input completeness, we defined a right-handed local coordinate system from the margin curve and opposing dentition. The target region was normalized to a domain with a half-extent of \(12\,\mathrm{mm}\). Each jaw was sampled at 4,096 points with normals, and the margin curve at 1,024 points. Sampling was controlled by a fixed random seed and case ID. Truncated signed distances were computed on a flexible dual grid.

%% 4.2 
\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}\label{subsec4_2}

\hspace{2em}To assess the geometric consistency between the predicted crowns and the reference CAD models, the two surfaces were compared using identical outer-surface segmentation, area-weighted sampling, and post-processing procedures. Each mesh was sampled at 50{,}000 points in physical coordinates. Following established dental crown evaluation protocols~\cite{Hosseinimanesh2025PointToMesh,Yang2024DCrownFormer,Yang2025DCrownFormerPlus,Wei2025VBCD,Wei2026MADCrowner}, the prespecified primary endpoints were symmetric CD-\(L_1\) and HD95. CD-\(L_1\) denotes the mean bidirectional nearest-neighbour distance, whereas HD95 is the larger directional 95th-percentile distance; lower values indicate better agreement. F-score@0.10\,mm, the harmonic mean of precision and recall within the tolerance, was a secondary coverage metric. Margin-to-surface P95 was the 95th-percentile distance from input margin points to the generated external surface, reflecting cervical geometric correspondence. Severe penetration was defined as a per-case signed distance below \(-0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\), with negative, zero, and positive values indicating interpenetration, contact, and clearance. The rate was the proportion of evaluable cases exceeding this threshold.

\hspace{2em}We additionally evaluated the support passed between the two flows on a ten-case held-out audit subset reserved for end-to-end inference visualization; this audit is separate from the 252-case headline surface evaluation. For each audit case, \(O\) denotes the reference active support obtained from the reference FDG latent at the Structure Flow grid resolution, and \(\hat O\) denotes the support obtained after Structure Flow occupancy selection with the fixed threshold \(\tau_{\mathrm{occ}}\). Support precision, recall, and F1 were defined as
\begin{equation}
P_O=\frac{|\hat O\cap O|}{|\hat O|},\qquad
R_O=\frac{|\hat O\cap O|}{|O|},\qquad
F1_O=\frac{2P_OR_O}{P_O+R_O},
\label{eq:support_metrics}
\end{equation}
with empty-set cases handled by the prespecified convention \(P_O=R_O=F1_O=1\) only when both supports are empty and zero otherwise. These quantities are averaged per case and reported as mean~\(\pm\)~SD rather than pooled over cells.

\hspace{2em}To isolate the effect of support mismatch on Feature Flow, the oracle-support control keeps the trained Feature Flow, condition features, per-case random seed, solver, step count, and decoder fixed, and runs the same model once with \(S=\hat O\) (the deployable setting) and once with \(S=O\). The paired difference in CD-\(L_1\), HD95, F-score, Margin P95, and severe-penetration rate estimates the geometric cost of Structure Flow support errors; oracle \(O\) is used only for this diagnostic and is never supplied in the main inference result. Results were summarized as case-level mean~\(\pm\)~standard deviation.

%% 4.3 
\subsection{Implementation Details}\label{subsec4_3}

\hspace{2em}Training was performed on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 GPU. The VAE, Structure Flow, and Feature Flow were optimized sequentially with AdamW, mixed precision, weight decay 0.01.Learning rates ranged from \(10^{-5}\) to \(10^{-4}\), with cosine decay after 1,000 warm-up steps. Structure Flow used eight gradient-accumulation steps. The Transformers used rotary position embeddings~\cite{Su2024RoFormer} and memory-efficient exact attention~\cite{Dao2022FlashAttention}. The structural autoencoder was frozen during Structure Flow training, while Feature Flow adapted the frozen Transformer backbone using rank-32 LoRA~\cite{Hu2022LoRA}. Checkpoints were selected based on validation performance. Flow training used \(\sigma=10^{-5}\) and auxiliary weights \((\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3,\lambda_4)=(0.6,0.35,3.0,0.5)\); velocity time weighting was \((1+2t^4)/1.4\), decoded supervision used \(t\leq0.75\), and endpoint supervision used four Euler steps.

\hspace{2em}At inference, one crown candidate was generated per case using a fixed case-specific seed. Structure Flow and Feature Flow used 50-step Heun and Euler solvers, respectively, with a classifier-free guidance scale of 3.0~\cite{Ho2022ClassifierFree}. The frozen VAE decoded the generated latents into oriented signed-FDG attributes, which were directly converted into the proposed FDG mesh. Post-processing included boundary cleanup, largest-component retention, and mesh cleaning. Final meshes were checked for watertightness, consistent orientation, single-component genus-0 topology, and geometric quality.

\textbf{Geometric and Static Spatial Evaluation.} As shown in Table~\ref{tab:main_comparison}, the proposed model achieved the highest geometric fidelity among the evaluated methods, with lower surface and cervical margin errors and a higher F-score. Fig.~\ref{fig:method_comparison_preview} illustrates these geometric differences across representative cases, showing fewer pronounced surface deviations and local artifacts around cusps, central grooves, and cervical margins.

\bigskip

\textbf{Support-mismatch analysis.} The reported deployable result uses \(S=\hat O\), whereas Feature Flow is optimized with \(S=O\). We evaluated the support produced by the deployed Structure Flow on the ten held-out cases used for the end-to-end inference audit, using the same 50-step Heun solver and zero occupancy threshold as inference. Case-level support precision was \(78.85\pm3.42\%\), recall was \(79.68\pm3.48\%\), F1 was \(79.25\pm3.30\%\), and IoU was \(65.75\pm4.60\%\) (mean\(\pm\)SD, \(n=10\)). Thus, on this audit subset, approximately one fifth of reference support cells were omitted (false negatives), and approximately one fifth of predicted support cells were extra (false positives). These measurements quantify a non-negligible support mismatch; they are a diagnostic subset analysis and are not generalized to all 252 test cases. We did not use oracle \(O\) for the headline results, so the support precision/recall analysis is the reported diagnostic of this mismatch. The headline surface metrics therefore should not be interpreted as evidence that Feature Flow is insensitive to support errors. In particular, omitted support cells cannot be recovered by the fixed-support Feature Flow; any such recovery would require a separate support-refinement mechanism.

\bigskip

\textbf{Technician and Workflow Evaluation.} An additional 80 eligible posterior single-crown cases were consecutively included according to the prespecified inclusion criteria, with no case overlap with the training, validation, or test sets. These criteria comprised complete maxillary and mandibular scans, a clearly delineated preparation margin, an available FDI designation, and valid occlusal registration. Three dental technicians, each with at least five years of crown design experience, independently assessed each generated design on a five-level ordinal scale ranging from unusable to no modification (Fig.~\ref{fig:technician_evaluation}). Overall, 79.2\% of the 240 ratings indicated no or only minor modifications, supporting the perceived usability of the generated crowns as CAD drafts for posterior single-crown restorations.

\textbf{Ablation of Hierarchical Generation.} This experiment tests whether separating global support prediction from local feature generation improves the reconstruction of posterior crowns. As shown in Table~\ref{table_hierarchical_ablation}, the two-stage model uses Structure Flow to predict active support and Feature Flow to generate local features, whereas the single-stage model jointly predicts both with one conditional flow network. Because single-stage training must balance global occupancy and fine geometric detail simultaneously, it increases surface errors, with HD95 particularly sensitive to local omissions and anomalies and F-score reduced by lower surface coverage. When the Structure Flow stage is removed, the model can still produce a plausible overall crown morphology but lacks explicit spatial support, which may leave empty regions near adjacent teeth, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation_adjacent}. Both configurations use the same inputs, decoder, data split, training budget, random seeds, and post-processing.

%% 4.4.3 Discussion
\subsubsection{Discussion}\label{subsubsec4_4_3}

\noindent\textbf{Interpretation of Results.}
The proposed framework combines geometric precision with dental context to jointly address anatomical fidelity and spatial compatibility. Oriented signed-FDG encodes surface distance and orientation, while the sparse VAE supports high-resolution reconstruction through a compact latent representation. Structure Flow predicts global occupancy support, enabling Feature Flow to concentrate on anatomical details within active regions. Multiscale conditioning guides both stages using tooth identity, cervical-margin geometry, and surrounding dentition, while decoded-surface supervision directly links latent learning to reconstruction accuracy. These complementary mechanisms provide a coherent explanation for the observed improvements in geometric accuracy and cervical-margin consistency, together with reduced antagonist penetration.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations and Scope of Application.}
Inadequate tooth preparation, incomplete dental scans, and atypical morphology remain risk scenarios. Fig.~\ref{fig:false_cases} shows that, despite preserved global crown form, failures concentrate in occlusal pits and fissures, including interrupted grooves, hole-like depressions, fragments, and spike-like protrusions, possibly because no explicit pit-and-fissure constraint is imposed. Direct FDG extraction may also be sensitive to inconsistencies in local geometry and connectivity, resulting in sliver faces or protrusions that post-processing cannot fully remove. Because the support is fixed before Feature Flow, a local Structure-Flow omission is another plausible source of a missing patch; the support audit above indicates that this mechanism is plausible but does not establish its case-level effect on surface metrics. The audit used ten held-out cases, so support precision, recall, and their relationship to geometric error should be confirmed on the complete test set. The system should therefore be used as a CAD draft for external crown morphology, with inspection and correction before downstream use; the reference CAD should be regarded as a historical design instance rather than unique ground truth. Future work should investigate controllable editing of localized crown details (e.g., cusps, ridges, grooves, and fossae), explicit constraints on pit-and-fissure continuity and local surface topology, support refinement between the two flows, and surface texture modeling beyond geometry alone, followed by independent laboratory validation, \textit{in vitro} measurements, and prospective workflow studies.

%% 5
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec5}

\hspace{2em}We present a case-conditioned framework for generating the external morphology of tooth-supported posterior single crowns. The oriented signed-FDG representation and a sparse VAE efficiently encode local geometry, signed distance, and oriented normals within the crown surface neighborhood, while two-stage Structure and Feature Flows conditional flow matching separately models global support and local anatomic detail. Global, voxel-aligned, and coordinate-neighborhood conditions further integrate maxillary and mandibular scans, a three-dimensional margin line, and FDI tooth-position information, allowing tooth-type priors, spatial arch relationships, and local boundary constraints to be incorporated during generation. Together with direct FDG mesh extraction, mesh cleanup, and engineering quality control, the framework establishes an end-to-end pathway from case inputs to candidate triangular meshes and provides a technical basis for high-resolution, editable, case-adapted crown design. Future work will extend the framework to broader tooth positions, missing-tooth patterns, and restorative settings; enable controllable editing of local crown details; incorporate surface texture information in addition to geometry; incorporate dynamic occlusion, manufacturing constraints, and human-in-the-loop design information; and investigate its use for anterior crowns, implant-supported restorations, and multi-unit prostheses to strengthen integration with digital restorative workflows.
