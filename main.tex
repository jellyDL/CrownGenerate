\begin{abstract}
Personalized dental crown design is a critical component of digital restorative workflows for structurally compromised teeth. Conventional computer-aided design (CAD) relies on iterative template adjustments to accommodate patient-specific anatomy, making the process time-consuming and operator-dependent. Although deep learning has automated parts of this workflow but has yet to reconcile high-frequency anatomical fidelity with dentition-level occlusal constraints. To address these limitations, we propose a case-conditioned generative framework for posterior single crowns. Given full-arch maxillary and mandibular scans, a three-dimensional preparation margin line, and the FDI tooth designation, it directly generates a candidate crown mesh. The approach employs a Flexible Dual Grid (FDG) representation with oriented signed-distance and surface-normal fields, while a sparse variational autoencoder (VAE) compresses high-resolution geometry into a compact latent space. Structure Flow and Feature Flow then sequentially generate crown occupancy and local surface features. Multiscale dental conditioning and surface supervision help preserve fine anatomical features and marginal continuity. Evaluation on an in-house dataset showed that the proposed method achieved the lowest \(L_1\) Chamfer distance among the evaluated methods (\(0.126 \pm 0.027\,\mathrm{mm}\)), demonstrating improved geometric fidelity and spatial compatibility. Independent technician assessments further supported the usability of the generated crowns as CAD drafts for posterior single-crown restorations.
\end{abstract}

%% 1. Introduction
\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}
\setlength{\parindent}{0pt}

\hspace{2em}Designing the external morphology of tooth-supported posterior single crowns is fundamental to digital restorative dentistry. This task poses a challenging conditional three-dimensional shape generation problem~\cite{neupane2026high,chen2026sv,han2027progressive}. The crown must accurately reproduce fine anatomical features, including cusps, ridges, grooves, and fossae, while maintaining a continuous contour along the manually delineated three-dimensional margin line. It must also preserve appropriate spatial relationships with adjacent and opposing teeth under static occlusion. Existing computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) systems typically require dental technicians to select a standard tooth template and iteratively position, deform, and sculpt it based on intraoral scans, making the process time-consuming and operator-dependent~\cite{Hosseinimanesh2025Thesis,Chen2024PreciseTooth,Wei2026MADCrowner}. These limitations hinder consistent patient-specific customization and broader clinical adoption of digital restorative workflows.

\hspace{2em}Recent advances in artificial intelligence have enabled data-driven methods for automated dental crown design. Early approaches formulated occlusal-surface reconstruction as image generation from two-dimensional depth maps~\cite{Tian2022DentalRecNet}. Subsequent point-cloud, voxel-based, and template-deformation methods extended prediction to three-dimensional crown geometry while incorporating case-specific anatomy and boundary information~\cite{Hosseinimanesh2025PointToMesh,Yang2024DCrownFormer,Wei2025VBCD}. Collectively, these studies have advanced crown generation from isolated shape prediction toward condition-aware reconstruction.

\hspace{2em}Meanwhile, general-purpose three-dimensional generative techniques have opened new avenues for high-resolution geometric synthesis. Flow matching learns generative transformations between distributions~\cite{lipman2023flow}, while structured latent representations disentangle sparse spatial structure from local geometric features~\cite{Xiang2025TRELLIS,xiang2026native}. Surface-neighborhood representations and sparse computation reduce the cost of high-resolution modeling compared with dense volumetric representations~\cite{He2025SparseFlex,Wu2025Direct3DS2}. Image-conditioned methods enable the reconstruction and synthesis of three-dimensional assets from visual inputs~\cite{Tochilkin2024TripoSR,Zhao2025Hunyuan3D20,Hunyuan3DTeam2025Hunyuan3D21}. Single-view Gaussian-splatting approaches have also been explored for three-dimensional object editing~\cite{xue2026SVGS}. These advances provide strategies for compressing geometric information, separating global structure from local detail, and conditioning generation on external observations.

\hspace{2em}Despite these advances, posterior crown generation remains geometrically underdetermined. The preparation margin and surrounding dentition provide only partial constraints, admitting multiple anatomically plausible morphologies for the same case~\cite{Ye2027CrownFusion}. The task therefore requires conciling high-frequency occlusal anatomy with cervical-boundary conformity and dentition-level spatial constraints. Depth projections lose axial and occluded geometry~\cite{Tian2022DentalRecNet}, point-based methods rely on sampling and surface reconstruction~\cite{Yang2025DCrownFormerPlus}, and volumetric methods trade resolution against computational cost~\cite{Wei2025VBCD}. Template deformation remains sensitive to the initial crown shape~\cite{Wei2026MADCrowner}, while general-purpose generators lack mechanisms to enforce these case-specific constraints. These limitations can yield geometrically plausible crowns that violate marginal, occlusal, or proximal constraints.

\hspace{2em}Unlike previous methods, we develop a unified generative framework that couples high-resolution surface modeling with multiscale patient-specific conditioning. The sparse Flexible Dual Grid (FDG) encodes signed distances and oriented normals near the crown surface, overcoming the resolution--cost bottleneck of dense volumetric representations. Structure Flow and Feature Flow decouple global support prediction from local feature synthesis, while multiscale dental conditioning and dual-level supervision coordinate fine anatomy with marginal and dentition-level spatial constraints. These designs enable the generation of geometrically faithful and spatially compatible patient-specific crowns.

\hspace{2em}The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

\hspace{1em}• Introduce an FDG representation augmented with truncated signed distances and oriented surface normals in high-resolution sparse cells. A sparse variational autoencoder (VAE) subsequently compresses this representation into a compact latent space.

\hspace{1em}• Develop a two-stage conditional flow-matching framework comprising Structure Flow and Feature Flow, which model sparse occupancy and local geometric features, respectively. Dual-level supervision in the latent space and on decoded surfaces preserves geometric details and surface continuity.

\hspace{1em}• Propose a multiscale dental conditioning mechanism that integrates the maxillary and mandibular dentitions, the margin line, and the FDI tooth designation at the global, voxel-aligned, and coordinate-neighborhood levels, thereby coordinating tooth-position semantics, margin geometry, and dentition-level spatial relationships.

% \hspace{1em}• Validate the method through geometric evaluations and independent technician assessments on an in-house dataset. Compared with the baselines, it achieves better geometric consistency and spatial compatibility, with favorable ratings for design quality and laboratory usability.

\hspace{2em}The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec2} reviews data-driven dental crown generation, high-resolution three-dimensional geometry modeling, and dental spatial conditioning. Section~\ref{sec3} presents the proposed framework, including oriented signed-FDG representation learning, sparse VAE compression, multiscale dental conditioning, two-stage conditional flow matching, and training objectives. Section~\ref{sec4} describes the dataset, preprocessing, evaluation metrics, implementation details, experimental results, and discussion. Section~\ref{sec5} concludes the paper and discusses directions for future work.

% Accordingly, this study focuses on the generation of a case-adapted CAD draft mesh conditioned on registered full-arch maxillary and mandibular scans, a three-dimensional margin line, and the FDI tooth designation. The scope is limited to the external morphology of tooth-supported posterior single crowns and excludes intaglio-surface design, cement-space definition, manufacturing compensation, and fabrication-ready restoration generation.

\subsection{Data-Driven Dental Crown Morphology Generation}\label{subsec2_1}

\hspace{2em}Previous learning-based methods often formulate crown design as two-dimensional occlusal-surface reconstruction. DentalRecNet uses occlusal depth projections and dual discriminators to reconstruct defects in mandibular first molars~\cite{Tian2022DentalRecNet}. However, this representation loses geometric information from axial surfaces, cervical margins, and occluded regions, while image-space metrics cannot adequately characterize the crown's three-dimensional relationship with the surrounding dentition.

\hspace{2em}Among these approaches, point-cloud completion and point-to-mesh methods predict crown points and surface normals from preparation and antagonist inputs, followed by differentiable surface reconstruction to obtain the final mesh~\cite{Yang2025DCrownFormerPlus}. Voxel-based methods incorporate curvature, margin, and FDI tooth-position information into volumetric crown generation~\cite{Wei2025VBCD}. Template-deformation methods combine cervical-margin information with multiscale anatomical conditioning and coarse-to-fine deformation of an initial tooth template~\cite{Wei2026MADCrowner}. These approaches demonstrate the value of dental context and boundary information; however, they remain subject to trade-offs involving sampling resolution, surface reconstruction, template dependence, local anatomical detail, and computational cost.

\hspace{2em}Implicit-field and diffusion models have further advanced dental shape completion. ToothDIT constructs individualized implicit templates from complete anterior-tooth morphology, whereas ToothCraft performs conditional diffusion over signed-distance-function voxels~\cite{Pukanec2026ToothCraft}. Several other studies investigate point-cloud completion and generative restoration for dental crowns~\cite{Chanintonsongkhla2025PointFlow,Ying2026Tooth3dNet,Shu2026AIDrivenCrown,Zhao2026ToothGAN,tian2026DM-CFO}. However, anterior-tooth template or synthetic-data evaluations may limit applicability to posterior crowns with prepared abutments and surrounding dentition.

\subsection{High-Resolution Three-Dimensional Geometry Generation}\label{subsec2_2}

\hspace{2em}High-resolution three-dimensional generation requires a balance among representational capacity, topological flexibility, and computational cost. Dense signed-distance-field (SDF) and occupancy representations can express complex shapes, but their storage and computational demands scale cubically with resolution. Marching Cubes extracts a surface from these fields but does not learn mesh connectivity directly~\cite{Lorensen1987MarchingCubes}. Sparse generative representations address this cost by separating global structure from local geometric features and restricting computation to relevant spatial neighborhoods~\cite{li2026pixal3d}. These methods provide transferable strategies for high-resolution representation and computationally efficient geometric generation; however, they do not explicitly model dental conditions or preparation-margin boundaries.

\hspace{2em}Explicit mesh-generation methods directly represent geometry and connectivity, providing an alternative paradigm for mesh synthesis. MeshFlow encodes continuous vertex positions and discrete connectivity relations in a latent space and generates them using rectified flow~\cite{Li_2026_CVPR}. LATO uses topology-preserving latents for mesh flow matching~\cite{Zhao2026LATO}, whereas FACE uses face-level autoregressive decoding~\cite{Wang2026FACE}. ShapeAsPoints reconstructs surfaces from oriented points using a differentiable Poisson solver~\cite{Peng2021ShapeAsPoints}. Other approaches use signed-distance fields and orientation-aware information, such as surface normals, for geometric supervision~\cite{li2025triposg,Ye2025Hi3DGen}. These studies indicate that oriented surface information is an important complement to structural representations for capturing high-frequency geometry.

%% 2.3
\subsection{Dental Spatial Conditioning and Evaluation}\label{subsec2_3}

\hspace{2em}Dental conditions serve distinct roles in crown generation. The three-dimensional preparation-margin line provides a boundary reference for the cervical contour; adjacent teeth and the opposing dentition provide proximal and static geometric constraints, respectively; and the FDI tooth designation supplies tooth-type and arch-related morphological priors. Hierarchical features and bidirectional sequence modeling also help delineate individual teeth in dental images~\cite{zhao2026innovative}. Prior crown-generation methods incorporate selected conditions at the input, loss, or post-processing stages~\cite{Wei2026MADCrowner,Yang2025DCrownFormerPlus,Wei2025VBCD}. However, existing studies have yet to consistently integrate tooth-position semantics, dentition-level spatial correspondences, and margin-neighborhood geometry within a unified multiscale framework.

\hspace{2em}Crown-design evaluation should jointly assess geometric fidelity, dental spatial compatibility, and laboratory usability. Prior studies report distance-based measures of surface deviation~\cite{Ying2026Tooth3dNet}, margin segmentation accuracy and proximal contact area~\cite{Wei2026MADCrowner}, antagonist proximity and surface intersections~\cite{Ye2027CrownFusion}, and design time and dentist ratings~\cite{Bae2026CrownGen}. Differences in distance definitions, coordinate normalization, and numerical scaling can make direct comparisons across studies misleading~\cite{Pukanec2026ToothCraft}. We therefore combine surface-distance, margin, and static geometric-proximity metrics with technician assessments to evaluate overall geometry, margin fidelity, spatial relationships, and required modifications.

\hspace{2em}Collectively, the reviewed studies indicate three unresolved requirements for posterior crown generation: a representation that preserves high-resolution local geometry, including partially occluded surfaces; a conditioning mechanism that couples preparation-margin, tooth-position, and dentition-level spatial context; and an evaluation protocol that distinguishes surface fidelity, spatial compatibility, and practical usability. Sections~\ref{sec3} and~\ref{sec4} describe the proposed framework and its evaluation, respectively.

\subsection{Overview}\label{subsec3_1}

%% 3.2
\subsection{Sparse Geometric Encoding}\label{subsec3_2}

%% 3.2.1
\subsubsection{Oriented Signed-FDG Representation}\label{subsubsec3_2_1}

\hspace{2em}The FDG stores geometric information only in active cells near the crown surface, avoiding dense high-resolution voxel grids~\cite{xiang2026native}. For active cell \(j\), \(c_j\) denotes its integer grid coordinate and \(x_j\) its center in the aligned physical domain. Standard FDG records the local dual-grid vertex offset \(v_j\in[0,1]^3\) and three axis-aligned intersection indicators \(b_j\in\{0,1\}^3\). These attributes encode geometry and connectivity but lack explicit signed-distance and normal cues, potentially hindering surface-side discrimination, orientation learning, and fine-detail reconstruction.

\hspace{2em}We therefore augment the FDG attributes with a truncated signed distance and an oriented normal. Before nearest-surface queries, reference meshes are cleaned and consistently oriented. In Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_oriented_signed_fdg_detail}, let \(p_j\) be the closest point on the reference surface to \(x_j\), \(n_j^f\) the unit normal of the closest face, and \(n_j\) the normalized, barycentrically interpolated vertex normal at \(p_j\), set to zero if the interpolated normal is degenerate. With physical grid spacing \(\Delta\) and dimensionless truncation bound \(\tau\), the truncated signed distance is defined as:
\begin{equation}
d_j=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(
\operatorname{sign}\!\left[(x_j-p_j)^\top n_j^f\right]
\frac{\|x_j-p_j\|_2}{\Delta},-\tau,\tau
\right),\label{eq:oriented_signed_distance}
\end{equation}
where \(d_j\) is expressed in grid-spacing units, \(\|\cdot\|_2\) is the Euclidean norm, and \((\cdot)^\top\) denotes transpose. The sign function determines the local surface side from the nearest-face normal, and clipping restricts the normalized distance to \([-\tau,\tau]\).

\hspace{2em}For each active cell, we concatenate the vertex offset, intersection indicators, truncated signed distance, and oriented normal into a local feature vector:
\begin{equation}
g_j=[v_j-0.5,\ b_j-0.5,\ d_j,\ n_j]\in\mathbb{R}^{10},
\label{eq:oriented_signed_feature}
\end{equation}
where subtracting \(0.5\) centers the offset and intersection channels. These attributes encode local geometry and surface orientation within the sparse neighborhood. Together with the active cell coordinates \(c_j\), they form the oriented signed-FDG representation used as input to the sparse geometric VAE.

%% 3.2.2
\subsubsection{Sparse VAE for Latent Compression}\label{subsubsec3_2_2}

\hspace{2em}The sparse geometric VAE compresses the high-resolution oriented signed-FDG representation into a latent for generative modeling. Its multilevel encoder progressively reduces the active support. The symmetric decoder reconstructs vertex offsets, axial-intersection logits, truncated signed distances, and normalized normals at the original resolution. The bottleneck latent \(Z=(O,F)\) comprises the active latent-grid support \(O\) and its local feature field \(F\). Structure Flow predicts \(O\), Feature Flow generates \(F\) on this support. The decoder predicts quadrilateral triangulation weights for differentiable FDG mesh extraction. During generation, the frozen decoder reconstructs crown geometry from the predicted latent.

\subsection{Multiscale Dental Conditions}\label{subsec3_3}

\hspace{2em}This section describes the multiscale dental conditions used by the two generation stages. After alignment and sampling, the maxillary, mandibular, and margin point sets are processed by independent condition encoders for Structure Flow and Feature Flow, indexed by \(d\in\{S,F\}\). Thus, \(G_S\) and \(V_S\) denote the global and voxel-aligned conditions for Structure Flow, while \(G_F\) and \(V_F\) denote those for Feature Flow. Maxillary and mandibular points use positions and surface normals, while margin points use positions only. Source-specific pointwise multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) with learned source embeddings encode each point set. The FDI code identifies the tooth quadrant and position; its embedding is added to the global condition \(G_d\). These global, voxel-aligned, and neighborhood conditions provide complementary case-level, spatial, and local context.

\bigskip

\textbf{Global Condition $G_d$.} Features from each point set are aggregated using mean and max pooling, concatenated, and projected into a case-level vector. The FDI code, which specifies the tooth quadrant and position, is embedded and added to this vector to form $G_d$, providing overall dental context and tooth identity.

\bigskip

\textbf{Voxel-Aligned Condition \(V_d\).} Point features are assigned to a voxel grid according to their coordinates, aggregated within each cell, and refined by local convolutions. The resulting multiscale features provide spatially aligned dental context to the flow stages, complementing the global condition.

\bigskip

\textbf{Neighborhood Condition $N(\hat{O})$.} For each query coordinate $q$, the Feature Flow encoder retrieves nearby maxillary, mandibular, and margin points. An MLP encodes their relative positions, normals, and distances into a local descriptor. At inference, descriptors at the generated support form $N(\hat{O})$, providing local context to Feature Flow.

%% 3.4
\subsection{Two-Stage Conditional Flow Matching}\label{subsec3_4}

\hspace{2em}The two-stage generation architecture shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_feature_condition_detail} decomposes crown generation into support prediction and local feature generation. Structure Flow predicts the active latent-grid support \(\hat O\), and Feature Flow generates the local feature field \(\hat F\) on this support. Here, \(O\) and \(\hat O\) denote the reference and predicted active supports, respectively. The stages use independent condition encoders and velocity networks while sharing the flow-matching probability path defined in Sec.~\ref{subsec3_5}.

%% 3.4.1
\subsubsection{Structure Flow}\label{subsec3_4_1}

\hspace{2em}Structure Flow generates the sparse support for subsequent feature generation (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_feature_condition_detail}, top). The multiscale encoder maps the maxillary, mandibular, and margin inputs to global and spatial features, with the FDI embedding incorporated into the global condition. These features reach the Structure Diffusion Transformer (DiT)~\cite{peebles2023scalable} via two pathways. The projected global condition and coarse voxel tokens provide case-level and spatial context for dental cross-attention. Spatial features are also pooled onto the latent grid and linearly projected into each DiT block.

\hspace{2em}The noisy structural latent \(\hat{x}_t^S\) is flattened into tokens. Structure DiT processes these tokens through time-modulated self-attention, dental cross-attention, and feed-forward layers. Within each block, aligned features are added to the cross-attention residual. The output projection maps the tokens back to the latent grid to predict the velocity field. The ordinary differential equation (ODE) solver integrates this field from Gaussian noise to obtain \(\hat{x}_O^S\). Here, \(\hat{x}_t^S\) is the noisy latent at flow time \(t\), and \(\hat{x}_O^S\) is the denoised latent. After denormalization, the frozen structure decoder~\cite{Xiang2025TRELLIS,xiang2026native} converts the latent into occupancy scores. Occupancy selection produces the active support \(\hat{O}\), which defines the coordinates for Feature Flow.

%% 3.4.2
\subsubsection{Feature Flow}\label{subsec3_4_2}

\hspace{2em}Feature Flow generates local latent features on the active support via condition fusion, Feature DiT, and ODE integration (Fig.~\ref{fig:figure_feature_condition_detail}, bottom). It operates on the support \(S\) at \(q\in S\), with \(S=O\) in training and \(S=\hat{O}\) at inference. Noisy features \(x_t^F(q)\) first pass through a residual adapter. In the residual-injection branch, linear maps project the global condition and voxel features at \(q\). In the local-modulation branch, MLPs encode dental neighborhood geometry and structural context from query positions and axial occupancy. Both branches add to the adapted features, forming fused tokens that combine dental and structural context.

\hspace{2em}The fused tokens enter Feature DiT with their coordinates. Sparse self-attention exchanges information within the support, while dental cross-attention accesses global and coarse voxel tokens from the condition encoder. Time modulation conditions the flow time. Feature DiT and its output adapter predict a velocity at each coordinate. The ODE solver integrates these velocities from Gaussian noise with fixed support. Omitted cells therefore cannot be recovered, and falsely activated cells may add spurious surface elements. The resulting features are denormalized to obtain \(\hat F\). At inference, $\hat F$ pairs with $\hat O$ to form $\hat Z=(\hat O,\hat F)$ for frozen VAE decoding. Here $x_t^F(q)$ is the noisy feature latent at $q$ and $t$, $\hat F$ denotes the generated local feature field, and $\hat Z$ denotes the predicted support--feature representation.


%% 3.5
\subsection{Training Objectives}\label{subsec3_5}

\hspace{2em}The sparse geometric VAE, Structure Flow, and Feature Flow are optimized sequentially, with stage-specific objectives formulated as follows.

%% 3.5.1
\subsubsection{VAE Reconstruction and Geometric Regularization}\label{subsubsec3_5_1}

\hspace{2em}The VAE reconstructs oriented signed-FDG attributes using an objective regularized by Kullback--Leibler (KL) divergence to preserve local geometry and surface orientation:
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{VAE}} = L_{\mathrm{attr}} + \lambda L_{\mathrm{KL}}
+ L_{\mathrm{surf}} + L_{\mathrm{smooth}} + L_{\mathrm{cons}},
\label{eq:vae_training_objective}
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda\) weights the KL term. \(L_{\mathrm{attr}}\) supervises geometry and connectivity via vertex-offset mean squared error (MSE), intersection and subdivision binary cross-entropy (BCE), and quadrilateral-midpoint error. \(L_{\mathrm{KL}}\) aligns the latent posterior with a standard normal prior~\cite{Kingma2013AutoEncoding}. \(L_{\mathrm{surf}}\) supervises distances and normals via smooth \(L_1\) loss~\cite{Girshick2015FastRCNN} and \(1-\hat{n}^{\top}n\) for normals \(\hat{n},n\). \(L_{\mathrm{smooth}}\) applies the same penalty to axial and Laplacian errors in vertex, distance, and normal fields. \(L_{\mathrm{cons}}\) penalizes predicted--reference residual differences:
\begin{equation}
r_{ij}=d_i-d_j-\tfrac12(n_i+n_j)^{\top}(c_i-c_j),
\label{eq:distance_normal_residual}
\end{equation}
where \(i,j\) index neighboring active cells and \(c_i,d_i,n_i\) denote grid coordinates, grid-spacing-unit signed distances, and normalized normals. Detail reconstruction combines vertex/distance and normal errors. Reference-normal-variation weights highlight detail in the neighborhood, residual, and reconstruction terms. Settings are given in Sec.~\ref{subsec4_3}.

%% 3.5.2
\subsubsection{Structure Flow and Feature Flow Objectives}\label{subsec3_5_2}

\hspace{2em}Both stages learn the ODE velocity fields via the flow-matching path~\cite{lipman2023flow}:
\begin{equation}
x_t=(1-t)x_0+[\sigma+(1-\sigma)t]\epsilon,
\qquad u_t=(1-\sigma)\epsilon-x_0,
\label{eq:flow_training_path}
\end{equation}
where $x_0$ and $x_t$ are the target and noisy latents, $u_t$ is the target velocity, $t\in[0,1]$ is the flow time, and $\sigma$ is the residual noise scale. Here, \(\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\) denotes standard Gaussian noise.

\hspace{2em}Structure Flow minimizes velocity MSE in the structural latent space:
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{Structure}}
=\mathbb{E}_{x_0^S,t,\epsilon}
\left[
\operatorname{MSE}\!\left(
v_{\theta_S}(x_t^S,t;G_S,V_S),u_t^S
\right)
\right],
\label{eq:structure_training_objective}
\end{equation}
where \(v_{\theta_S}\) is the Structure DiT velocity field with parameters \(\theta_S\), conditioned on the features \(G_S, V_S\). The target \(x_0^S\), noisy \(x_t^S\), and velocity \(u_t^S\) follow Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow_training_path}. MSE averages over latent entries. At inference, the ODE solver integrates the predicted field. After denormalization, the frozen decoder and occupancy selection yield \(\hat{O}\).


\hspace{2em}Feature Flow minimizes a flow-matching (FM) loss on the reference support with latent and decoded supervision:
\begin{equation}
L_{\mathrm{Feature}}=L_{\mathrm{FM}}+\lambda_1L_{\mathrm{rec}}
+\lambda_2L_{\mathrm{grad}}+\lambda_3L_{\mathrm{dec}}+\lambda_4L_{\mathrm{end}},
\label{eq:feature_training_objective}
\end{equation}
where \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_4\) weight the auxiliary losses. \(L_{\mathrm{FM}}\) applies time-weighted MSE to \(v\), giving \(\hat{x}_0=(1-\sigma)x_t-[\sigma+(1-\sigma)t]v\). \(L_{\mathrm{rec}}\) and \(L_{\mathrm{grad}}\) compare \(\hat{x}_0\) with \(x_0\) in value and axial-neighbor differences via \((1-t)\)-weighted MSE; the velocity and reconstruction terms also use normalized target-neighborhood weights. \(L_{\mathrm{dec}}\) supervises frozen-VAE decodings of \(\hat{x}_0\) and \(x_0\) with \((1-t)\)-weighted detail, neighborhood, and distance--normal penalties and BCE on signs, intersections, and subdivision. \(L_{\mathrm{end}}\) is the Euler-endpoint MSE to \(x_0\).

\section{Experiments and Discussion}\label{sec4}

%% 4.1
\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing}\label{subsec4_1}

\hspace{2em}We assembled an in-house dataset of processed gypsum-model cases from multiple dental prosthetic laboratories and screened them for eligibility. Each eligible case comprised maxillary and mandibular gypsum models, a cervical margin line, an FDI designation, and a technician-designed reference CAD mesh. The dataset contained no patient-identifying or physiological information. Inclusion required at least four adjacent teeth, valid margin and FDI records, and a valid maxillomandibular occlusion; cases with incomplete or invalid required records were excluded. The final dataset comprised 5,049 eligible cases. These cases were randomly divided at the case level and stratified by FDI position, jaw, and laterality into 4,292 training, 505 validation, and 252 test cases. Case-level partitioning prevented overlap among the subsets, while stratification maintained comparable distributions of FDI position, jaw, and laterality. The test set included only posterior single crowns.

\hspace{2em}Preprocessing involved input validation, spatial alignment, and sampling. After verifying input completeness, we defined a right-handed local coordinate system from the margin curve and the opposing dentition. The target region was normalized to a domain with a half-extent of \(12\,\mathrm{mm}\). Each jaw was sampled at 4,096 points with associated normals, and the margin curve was sampled at 1,024 points. Sampling was controlled by a fixed random seed and the case identifier. Truncated signed distances were computed on a flexible dual grid.

%% 4.2
\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}\label{subsec4_2}

\hspace{2em}To assess geometric consistency, predicted and reference crowns were compared after identical segmentation, sampling, registration, and post-processing. Following cited protocols~\cite{Hosseinimanesh2025PointToMesh,Yang2024DCrownFormer,Yang2025DCrownFormerPlus,Wei2025VBCD}, surface-level agreement was measured by symmetric Chamfer distance (\(\mathrm{CD}\text{-}L_1\); mean bidirectional nearest-neighbor distance) and 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance (\(\mathrm{HD95}\); larger directional percentile), with lower values indicating closer agreement. Surface coverage was measured by the F-score, the harmonic mean of precision and recall within \(0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\), with higher values indicating better coverage. Cervical fidelity was measured by margin-to-surface 95th-percentile distance (Margin P95), the unsigned distance from sampled margin points to the generated surface. Occlusal compatibility was measured by the severe-penetration rate, the proportion of evaluable cases with minimum crown--antagonist signed distance below \(-0.10\,\mathrm{mm}\) under static occlusion. These metrics evaluate external crown morphology only and do not establish internal fit, dynamic occlusal performance, or patient outcomes.

%% 4.3
\subsection{Implementation Details}\label{subsec4_3}

\hspace{2em}The oriented signed-FDG representation used a grid resolution of \(R=1024\) and truncation parameter \(\tau=3\). The five-level sparse VAE used channel widths of 48, 96, 192, 384, and 512, producing 32-channel latents on a sparse \(64^3\) grid. Global conditions were projected to 512 dimensions, and voxel-aligned conditions used \(64^3\) and \(16^3\) grids with 7-dimensional neighborhood descriptors normalized by \(h=12\,\mathrm{mm}\). All models were optimized with Adam using mixed precision, a weight decay of 0.01, learning rates of \(10^{-5}\)--\(10^{-4}\), and cosine decay after 1,000 warm-up steps.

\hspace{2em}For the two flow networks, Transformer blocks used rotary embeddings and memory-efficient exact attention~\cite{Su2024RoFormer,Dao2022FlashAttention}, with the sparse VAE frozen and rank-32 LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} applied to Feature Flow. Flow training used \(\sigma=10^{-5}\), auxiliary weights \((0.6,0.35,3.0,0.5)\), and velocity weighting \((1+2t^4)/1.4\). At inference, one candidate per case was generated with a fixed seed, using 50 steps with the Heun and Euler solvers, respectively, and a classifier-free guidance scale of 3.0~\cite{Ho2022ClassifierFree}. The frozen VAE decoded latents into oriented signed-FDG attributes for direct mesh extraction, followed by mesh cleanup and checks of watertightness, orientation consistency, genus-0 topology, and geometric validity.

%% 4.4.3 Discussion
\subsubsection{Discussion}\label{subsubsec4_4_3}

\hspace{2em}Automated posterior crown generation is significant for digital restorative dentistry because it can reduce the manual effort required for template adjustment and improve the consistency of patient-specific designs. The proposed method outperformed the evaluated baselines in geometric fidelity and static spatial compatibility, and 79.2\% of the technician ratings indicated that the generated crowns required no or only minor modifications. The ablation results suggest that these improvements arise from separating global support prediction from local feature generation, using signed-distance and normal cues to describe local surfaces, and incorporating margin and antagonist information at different spatial scales. Nevertheless, inadequate preparations, incomplete scans, and atypical morphologies remain challenging, and localized pit-and-fissure artifacts and sliver faces may occur; together with the non-unique nature of technician-designed references, these limitations require generated crowns to be treated as CAD drafts requiring inspection rather than fabrication-ready restorations (Fig.~\ref{fig:false_cases}). Future work will investigate controllable editing of local crown details, explicit pit-and-fissure continuity constraints, and surface texture modeling, followed by independent laboratory validation and prospective workflow studies.

%% 5
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec5}

\hspace{2em}We present a case-conditioned framework for generating the external morphology of tooth-supported posterior single crowns. The oriented signed-FDG representation and a sparse VAE efficiently encode local geometry, signed distance, and oriented normals within the crown surface neighborhood, while two-stage conditional flow matching with Structure Flow and Feature Flow separately models global support and local anatomical detail. Global, voxel-aligned, and coordinate-neighborhood conditions further integrate maxillary and mandibular scans, a three-dimensional margin line, and FDI tooth-position information, allowing tooth-type priors, spatial arch relationships, and local boundary constraints to be incorporated during generation. Together with direct FDG mesh extraction, mesh cleanup, and engineering quality control, the framework establishes an end-to-end pathway from case inputs to candidate triangular meshes and provides a technical basis for high-resolution crown design suitable for subsequent CAD refinement. Future work will extend the framework to broader tooth positions, missing-tooth patterns, and restorative settings; enable controllable editing of local crown details; incorporate surface texture information in addition to geometry; incorporate dynamic occlusion, manufacturing constraints, and human-in-the-loop design information; and investigate its use for anterior crowns, implant-supported restorations, and multi-unit prostheses to strengthen integration with digital restorative workflows.
